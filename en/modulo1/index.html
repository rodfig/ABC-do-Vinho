Definition and Origin of Wine

Wine is the product obtained by the natural fermentation of fresh grape juice.

This simple definition contains within itself one of the oldest and noblest traditions of humanity, which dates back thousands of years and crosses all civilizations. Before being a drink, wine was currency, medicine, religious symbol and instrument of diplomacy.

Wine is not manufactured — it is produced. The transformation of grape juice into wine results of natural biological processes, carried out by yeasts present in the grape itself and in the cellar environment. Human beings have learned, over millennia, to how to conduct and improve this process.

The Millennial Origins

Archaeological evidence points to Georgia as the place where wine was most likely first produced, with grape pips dated between 8,000 and 5,000 BC. Pieces ceramics containing grape seeds and bitartrate were found in the Caucasus region — Georgia, Armenia, eastern Turkey and northern Iran.

~8000 BC
Caucasus — first traces

~6500 BC
Europe — first evidence

The oldest evidence of wine production in Europe, coming from sites archaeological sites in Greece.

~5500 BC

Neolithic farmers from the western Mediterranean arrive in the territory Portuguese. The cultivated vine would arrive much later.

7th century BC
Phoenicians and Greeks in the Iberian Peninsula

Diffusion of viticulture and wine trade throughout the Mediterranean until far west of Iberia.

Critical note

The idea that "15 thousand years ago" wine was made, associated with Flood biblical account and the Black Sea hypothesis, is popular but has no solid scientific basis. The so-called "Black Sea deluge hypothesis" is debated in geology but does not displace the archaeological evidence — which clearly begins in the 6th millennium BC. Wine history is ancient, but it is not 15,000 years old.

Expansion in Antiquity

In the Iron Age, Phoenicians and Greeks spread the viticulture and the wine trade across the Mediterranean. In the far west, the Greeks founded Emporion/Empúrias (Catalonia) in the 6th century BC, but on the Atlantic seaboard — in territory that would later be Portugal — the initial impulse came from Phoenician trade and, later, the romanization.

The Phoenicians

  • First major wine merchants in the Mediterranean
  • Established Atlantic routes to the Iberian Peninsula
  • Introduced storage techniques in amphorae
  • Main wine-growing expansion agent on the Portuguese coast

The Greeks

  • Founded colonies in the Mediterranean (Empurias, Marseille)
  • They associated wine with the cult of Dionysus (Roman Bacchus)
  • They elevated wine to almost sacred status
  • They introduced new Mediterranean varieties into the Iberian Peninsula

Trade with the Greeks brought improvements in winemaking techniques in the region. The Greeks didn't just trade wine — they associated it with worship, philosophy and gastronomy. The Greek symposium, a collective drinking ritual, was one of the first organized social contexts around wine.

Romanization and the Classical Apogee

From the 2nd century BC on, the Peninsula entered the orbit of Rome. With the romanization (2nd century BC until the 5th century AD) came agricultural techniques, pruning, training and pressing, presses and clay vats for winemaking and storage, in addition to logistics networks such as roads and ports. There is archaeological evidence of wine production in Douro in Roman times.

The Roman Soldier and Viticulture

One of the most effective ways to expansion of the vineyard was the Roman army. The soldier didn't just drink wine — he was an active agent in its production. The tripartite system of payment to the legions included wine as an essential component:

⅓
in Soldi
Roman coins for local purchases and personal needs
⅓
in Wine
The soldier's "pharmacy" — antiseptic, analgesic and tonic
⅓
in Salt
For food preservation and nutritional value
💡

The words pay and soldier originate from the name of the Roman coin solidus — confirming the direct link between remuneration and the military designation.

Wine as a Military Pharmacy

Wine performed multiple medical functions in Roman army:

  • Antiseptic — for cleaning wounds in the field
  • Painkiller — for pain relief
  • Tonic — for strength and endurance when walking
  • Preservative — for other foods and medicines

Spread by "Green Colonization"

The soldiers carried vine cuttings — shoot segments with 4 dormant gems, light, resistant, that fit in a quiver. Just bury them in soil suitable for creating a new vineyard. This strategy represented a way of agricultural colonization: each legionnaire was a potential wine grower, creating vineyards for local supply, reducing dependence on imports and integrating local populations in the Roman economy. The legionnaires functioned as true agents of agricultural development, planting vines from Brittany to North Africa.

According to Cato, in his treatise De Agri Cultura from the 2nd century BC, the best agricultural exploitation was the vineyard — demonstrating the strategic importance of Roman viticulture.

Christianity and the Arab Period

Barbarians and Christian Expansion

Between the 6th and 7th centuries, the invasions of the Suevi and Visigoths did not significantly interrupt wine production. Simultaneously, the expansion of Christianity conferred wine a new sacred meaning through the sacrament of Eucharist, where the wine represents the blood of Christ. This religious dimension was fundamental to preserve and develop viticulture during troubled periods — monasteries became the guardians of European viticulture and winemaking techniques.

The Paradox of the Arab Period (8th–12th Centuries)

The Emir's Paradox

The Arab invasion of the Iberian Peninsula brought a unique challenge: a people who, for religious precepts, did not consume wine. However, the Emir of Córdoba, who governed the Peninsula, allowed consumption by Christians and, surprisingly, wine continued to be produced and widely exported during this period. The Arabs, known for their advanced agricultural techniques, indirectly contributed to viticulture.

This coexistence reveals a lasting lesson: wine survived and thrived even in ideologically adverse contexts, thanks to its economic value and its cultural centrality for Christian populations. Exports remained active, keeping the Iberian wine tradition during four centuries of Arab rule.

The Birth of Portugal

In 1143, with the recognition of Portuguese independence, a new era began for national viticulture. The territorial conquest, completed in 1249 with the Algarve, allowed the installation of military and monastic religious orders that actively promoted agriculture.

Vine planting grew exponentially by diverse reasons: liturgical activities' needs, sustenance of religious communities and economic development. The monastic and military orders — especially the Cistercians — and royal donations encouraged vineyard planting, soil drainage and mills' construction, leaving marks in regions such as Beira, Minho, Bairrada, Ribatejo and Estremadura.

📜

The very founding act of nationality is linked to wine: when the king granted "lands good for wine," he implicitly acknowledged the economic and social importance of this culture.

Navigations and Wine

The 15th century marks a golden period in the history of Portuguese wine, intrinsically linked to the epic of the Discoveries. The Portuguese, who had fished for cod in the North since the 13th century, "turned" south, managing to reach India in 1498 with Vasco da Gama.

Wine was fundamental in this navigation "enterprise". Life on board depended on three essential elements: wine, salt and bread. Wine served multiple purposes on board:

🛡️
Conservation
Alcohol helped preserve the drink during months of travel
🌾
Nutrition
Provided essential calories and nutrients to the crew
⚕️
Medicine
Used as an antiseptic and tonic during long journeys

In the 17th century, Madeira gained Atlantic fame (including consumption in Americas and in the British colonies), while the Douro asserted itself as origin of the wines that would make Port Wine. In 1756, the Marquis of Pombal created the Companhia Geral da Agricultura dos Vinhos do Alto Douro — one of the first modern systems of delimitation and regulation of a wine region in the world.

The Methuen Treaty (1703)

The Methuen Treaty, also referred to as "Treaty of Cloth and Wine" or "Queen Anne's Treaty", was signed between England and Portugal December 27, 1703, and remained active until 1836. Its negotiators were John Methuen, on the British side, and D. Manuel Teles da Silva, on the Portuguese side.

The Terms of the Agreement

The treaty established an asymmetric bilateral commitment:

  • English woolen fabrics would be admitted to Portugal tax-free
  • Portuguese wines would have preferential rates on the English market
  • Portugal committed itself not to restrict the import of English manufactured goods
  • England guaranteed privileged access to Portuguese wines — to the detriment of French wines

Positive Impacts

  • Massive expansion of Portuguese wine exports
  • Development of viticulture, especially in Douro
  • Consolidation of the English commercial presence in Portugal

Negative Consequences

  • Deindustrialization of Portugal
  • Chronic trade deficit
  • Growing dependence on English manufactured goods
  • Structural economic subordination to England

The treaty established the basis for an asymmetric trade relationship that would last more than a century, making Portugal a supplier of raw materials (wine) and consumer of English manufactured goods.

The Pombaline Reforms (1750–1777)

The Marquis of Pombal (1699–1782), Prime Minister of D. José I, implemented revolutionary reforms that forever transformed the Portuguese wine industry. His strategy was to use Port Wine as an alternative to balance the deficit of the Portuguese state — Pombal, literally, paid for the reconstruction of Lisboa with Port wine.

The First Demarcated Region in the World

In September 10, 1756, by royal charter, the General Agricultural Company of Vinhas do Alto Douro, with the aim of disciplining wine production and trade in the region.

World historic landmark

The Douro Pombaline demarcation — marked by 335 stone cairns with the name "Feitoria" — preceded similar initiatives in France by 150 years. It was the first controlled denomination of origin in history.

Company Powers

  • Commercial Monopoly — total control over the marketing of wine from Porto
  • Quality Control — supervision of all production phases
  • Price Fixing — stabilization of purchase and sale prices
  • Technical Regulation — winemaking standards
  • Combating Fraud — elimination of adulterated wines

The Pombaline reforms revolutionized Portuguese viticulture, raising quality standards, gaining worldwide recognition for Port wine and becoming an inspiring model for other world wine regions.

The English and Portuguese Wine

The English presence in Portuguese wine marketing largely precedes the Methuen Treaty, dating back to the 16th century. Frequent conflicts with France made imports of French wines difficult, making Portuguese wines a natural alternative for the British market — appreciated for its quality, robustness and logistical proximity by sea.

The Commercial Domain (18th century)

During the 18th century, dozens of English trading houses established themselves in Porto, dominating the Port Wine trade and absorbing around 60–70% of total Douro production:

Strategies that Shaped the Sector

What did the English bring

  • Techniques for aging in cellars (aging)
  • Methods of controlled fortification
  • Systematic quality control
  • Modern business management and distribution networks
  • Pioneering marketing — developing international brands

What Portugal gained from the English

  • Access to global markets of the British Empire
  • Investment capital for infrastructure
  • Strict quality standards and international reputation

  • Portuguese wine vocabulary incorporated into English

English Consumption as a Driver

Port Wine has become a British cultural phenomenon:

  • Upper classes — symbol of social status, served after dinner
  • Royal Navy — official ration of British officers
  • Medicinal use — prescribed as a tonic and digestive
  • Social rituals — integrated into English hospitality habits

The Portuguese-British wine relationship is one of the longest and most productive areas of European history — shaped the world wine market and created the style that defines Port Wine to this day.

The Phyloxera Crisis and Reconstruction

In 1860–90, phylloxera — an American insect that attacks the roots of Vitis vinifera — devastated European vineyards. In Portugal, the impact was catastrophic in almost all regions.

The Portuguese exception

Colares, with its sandy soils, resisted phylloxera thanks to sand "barrier": the insect cannot survive in pure sand, so French foot vines survived intact. Colares is today one of the last regions in the world with ungrafted vines.

The solution found for the rest of Europe was grafting onto American rootstocks — roots resistant to phylloxera onto which the desired European variety is grafted. This method, still universal today, completely redesigned winemaking worldwide and led to massive replanting with the reconfiguration of varieties and vineyards.

The 19th century also brought powdery mildew (1852) and then phylloxera (1872), which together represented the biggest crisis in the history of European viticulture. The modern rebirth came with systematic replanting, the introduction of resistant varieties and the repositioning of Portuguese wine as a luxury product into international markets.

Portuguese Wine Classification System

The Portuguese classification system, integrated into European legislation, organizes wines into three large hierarchical categories, with increasing demands for regulation and quality control.

DOP / DOC

  • Maximum European quality category
  • Production restricted to geographically delimited areas
  • varieties authorized by region
  • Mandatory analytical control and organoleptic testing
  • Certification by Comissões Vitivinícolas Regionais

IGP / Regional Wine

  • Less restrictive than DOP/DOC
  • Minimum 85% grapes from the region
  • Allows varieties and unauthorized methods in DOC
  • Greater technical and stylistic freedom
  • In Portugal known as "Regional Wines"

The Third Category: Vinho

Wines that do not fall under DOC or IGP are simply designated Vinho (ex-Vinho de Mesa). They cannot indicate specific region of origin or vintage on the label, but they have complete freedom on varieties and techniques — sometimes becoming the choice of experimental producers.

Entities in the Wine Sector

The Portuguese wine sector is regulated and promoted by a network of public and interprofessional entities, with complementary functions of certification, regulation and promotion.

From Junta Nacional do Vinho to IVV

1933
Federação dos Vinicultores do Centro e Sul

First corporate body of the Estado Novo for regulating the national wine market.

1937
Junta Nacional do Vinho (JNV)

Created by Decree-Law No. 27,977. Functions: economic defense of small viticulture, price regularization, quality control and surplus management. From 1945 onwards, it created a network of cooperative wineries across the country.

1986
Instituto da Vinha e do Vinho (IVV)

Accession to the European Community required the replacement of the JNV by a body aligned with community market policies. The IVV started to regulate, certify, control and promote — abandoning the corporate model.

The Current Network of Entities

IVV
Instituto da Vinha e do Vinho
National regulation, certification, production and marketing control. Public institute under the supervision of the Ministry of Agriculture
CVR
Comissões Vitivinícolas Regionais
Certification and quality control by DOC. They issue guarantee seals and represent the regions at international events
ViniPortugal
Associação Interprofissional
International promotion under the brand "Wines of Portugal". Founded by 8 associations of commerce, production and demarcated regions

Specific Entities for Historical Denominations

IVDP

Instituto dos Vinhos do Douro e Porto. Certification and quality control for DOC Porto and DOC Douro. Generate the Conselho Interprofissional from Douro and Porto.

IRVV

Instituto do Vinho da Madeira (current IVBAM — Instituto do Vinho, do Bordado e do Artesanato da Madeira). Supervision of the entire Wine production of Madeira.

Wine and Health

The relationship between wine and health is as old as civilization itself. From biblical writings to modern medicine, wine was simultaneously celebrated as a tonic and debated as a risk — a duality that remains relevant in the 21st century.

Moderate consumption of wine has been associated with cardiovascular and digestive benefits. The WHO warned in 2023 that there is no safe level of alcohol consumption — a position that does not invalidate responsible consumption, but invites to conscience.

Wine Throughout the History of Medicine

Bible
Wedding of Cana and Digestion

Wine appears as a natural complement to the meal. Biblical references associate it with joy, sharing and physical well-being.

Rome
Military Pharmacy

Antiseptic, analgesic and tonic for legionnaires. Wine was considered essential medicine on the battlefield.

19th century
Medical Prescription

Port Wine was prescribed by English medicine as a tonic and digestive, especially for convalescents and women after childbirth.

20th–21st century
Epidemiological Studies

Scientific investigation identifies cardiovascular benefits of moderate consumption — the so-called "French paradox" has fueled decades of medical debate.

Five Dimensions of the Wine-Health Relationship

🍷
Digestion
Wine stimulates the production of saliva and gastric juices, aiding digestion when consumed with meals
❤️

Studies associate moderate consumption with a lower risk of heart disease — the antioxidants in red wine are central to this debate
🔬
Hygiene and Control
Modern viticulture and oenology follow strict hygiene and food safety standards, guaranteeing product quality

The Concept of "Wine at glass"

Portugal has developed a consumer culture that is, by nature, moderate: wine at glass — served in controlled doses at meals, integrated into a Mediterranean dietary pattern rich in olive oil, vegetables and fish. This form of consumption is radically different from binge drinking and corresponds to the profile of consumption that scientific studies associate with neutral or positive effects.

WHO Position (2023)

The World Health Organization declared that there is no safe level of alcohol consumption for human health. This statement is not new — alcohol is a group 1 carcinogen. What changes is emphasis on public communication. The producer and Sommelier have the responsibility to inform and the adult consumer has the responsibility to choose consciously.

History of the Sommellerie

The profession of Sommelier has ancestral roots that date back to the first civilizations organized. The function of taster of real drinks was simultaneously technical and security — ensuring that the sovereign's drink was not poisoned.

5th century BC
Biblical origins

Nehemiah, exiled to Persia after the destruction of Jerusalem, was made "cupbearer" (Escanção) of King Artaxerxes. Royal drinks taster function, vital to the monarch's safety.

16th century
Francis I of France

The king maintained an official cupbearer, establishing the tradition in the French court and elevating the role of Escanção to recognized noble function.

18th century
Lord Nelson and Porto

The British admiral had a young officer who specifically tasted the Port Wine for him — demonstrating how specific wines already required specialized knowledge.

70s
Professionalization in Portugal

Foundation of AEP (Associação dos Escanções de Portugal), marking the beginning of the professionalization of the sector. The Portuguese hospitality industry began to rely on qualified professionals, elevating sommellerie to a recognized art.

Wine as Cultural Heritage

The history of wine is, ultimately, the history of civilization itself. Das first spontaneous fermentations in Georgia 8,000 years ago to sophisticated techniques contemporary winemaking, wine remains a bridge between the past and the future, between tradition and modernity, between the land and the table.

The Portuguese Art of Blending

Portugal has developed a unique competence in creating wines with character through of the harmonious mixture of different varieties. This oenological philosophy privileges complementarity over varietal domination, creating wines of greater complexity and personality:

  • Port Wines — complex blend of multiple Douro varieties
  • Red wines from Douro — combinations of Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz
  • Vinhos Verdes — mix of regional varieties whites
  • DOC do Dão — Pairing between varieties traditional and specific microclimates

A Consolidated National Brand

With 31 DOCs, 14 IGPs and 250 indigenous varieties — the greatest varietal diversity in Europe —, Portugal consolidates itself as a global reference in denominations of origin. The combination of unique terroir, centuries-old tradition and modern regulation is the formula for lasting success in international viticulture.

Some of the noblest human creations stand the test of time and continue evolving, always maintaining its fundamental essence: the ability to transform simple grape juice into a unique sensory, cultural and civilizational experience.

Challenges and Future Perspectives

Portuguese wine reached the 21st century as one of the most competitive and recognized in the world. But the facing global viticulture challenges — climate, technology, market — require a strategic response that combines the richness of tradition with the agility of innovation.

Sustainability and Climate

  • Adaptation of varieties and cultivation practices to climate change
  • Sustainable and organic viticulture certifications
  • Reducing the carbon footprint in production and logistics
  • Preservation of indigenous varietal biodiversity

Digitization and Traceability

  • SIVV system — complete traceability from field to bottle
  • Digital promotion and e-commerce platforms
  • Automated analytical control in wineries
  • Interoperability with European certification systems

Innovation with Roots

The biggest strategic challenge is to maintain the balance between tradition and modernity: preserving the typicality that makes Portuguese wines unique in the global market, while adopting contemporary oenological techniques that respond to the tastes and demands of the 21st century consumer.

🌱

Sustainable viticulture is not just a response to the market — it is a need for survival. Climate projections indicate that Portuguese wine regions will face average temperatures 1.5–2°C higher by 2050, requiring adaptation of varieties, driving systems and oenological practices.

Portugal has everything the global market is looking for: authenticity, diversity, territorial identity and a history of millennia. The challenge is to tell this story to the world — with consistency, quality and ambition.
