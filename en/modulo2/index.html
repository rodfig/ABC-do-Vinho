Tradition, Technique and Market

Viticulture is simultaneously one of humanity's oldest agricultural practices and one of the most dynamic in the 21st century. Cultivating the vine requires in-depth knowledge of Terroir, varieties and agronomic techniques — and its evolution reflects both ancient traditions and recent scientific innovations.

Old World vs. New World

Old World

  • Europe, Middle East, North Africa
  • Denominations of origin as a central axis
  • Tradition and Terroir above variety
  • Strict production regulation
  • Examples: Portugal, France, Italy, Spain

New World

  • Americas, Oceania, South Africa
  • variety as main marketing element
  • Greater freedom in production
  • Intense technological innovation
  • Examples: Australia, Argentina, USA, Chile

Mechanization and Old Vines

Mechanization (mechanical harvesting, assisted pruning, vegetation management) reduces costs and allows large areas to be worked. However, vineyards with a steep slope — as in Douro or Minho — remain essentially manual.

Old vines (40, 60 or more than 80 years old) produce less quantity but grapes of greater concentration and complexity. Deep root systems access layers of soil inaccessible to young vines, expressing Terroir more fully.

Classification

In Portugal, the quality of wine is certified by Comissões Vitivinícolas Regionais (CVR), which manages designations of origin (DOP) and geographical indications (IGP). The pyramid goes from Vinho de Mesa → IGP → DOP, with the highest level requiring varieties, specific production practices and zones.

The Plant and the varieties

variety and Graft Holder

After the phylloxera devastation in the 19th century, all European vines began to be planted with the aerial part (European variety Vitis vinifera) grafted onto roots of American species resistant to the insect. This binomial variety + rootstock is now universal in viticulture.

The choice of rootstock influences the vine's vigor, maturity date, adaptation to soil and resistance to limestone. The wrong combination can compromise quality even with an excellent variety.

Cultivation and Propagation

The vine propagates mainly by stake (shoot segment with 2–4 buds) or diving. Vegetative propagation ensures that each plant is genetically identical to the parent plant — essential for maintaining varietal characteristics.

In vineyard installation, soil is stripped and prepared; the rootstock is planted and, after one year, it is grafted with the desired variety. The vineyard only starts producing quality grapes from the 4th–5th year onwards.

Clones

One clone is an individual selected within a variety, with specific characteristics of production, health or aromatic profile. Clonal selection makes it possible to reproduce the best plants in a vineyard without losing genetic variability in variety.

Example — Alvarinho

variety Alvarinho has clones numbered (44, 45, 46, 47) with different behaviors in terms of vigor, fertility index and aromatic profile. Choosing the correct clone is part of each producer's viticultural strategy.

Vineyard Care

The way the vineyard is treated throughout the year defines its physiological balance, the health of the grapes and, ultimately, the quality of the wine. There are five main approaches, with distinct environmental and qualitative implications.

Vine Pruning

Legend has it that it was a donkey who taught winegrowers how to prune — by gnawing the shoots of a vine, it produced more and better the following season. In any case, pruning is the most decisive operation for the balance between production and quality.

Short Pruning (Cordon)

  • Spurs with 2–3 buds
  • Lower vegetative vigor
  • Facilitates mechanization
  • Suitable for varieties high basal fertility

Mixed Pruning (Guyot)

  • One long stick (6–12 buds) + renewal spur
  • Greater production
  • Suitable for low basal fertility varieties
  • Common in Vinho Verde and Alentejo

The empa (or piecing) consists of bending and tying the long stick horizontally to the wire, uniformizing the bursting of the buds along the entire stick. A summer pruning (depamping, defoliation, topping) complements winter pruning by managing vegetation during growth.

Vine Vegetative Cycle

The vine follows a well-defined annual cycle, from sap bleeding in March to dormancy in December. Each phase has direct implications for the quality of the harvest and requires specific interventions from the winegrower.

Feb–Mar
Bleeding

After pruning, sap flows through the cut wounds — a sign that the vine has "awakened." Indicates resumption of root activity.

Mar–Apr
Budbreak

The buds open and the first shoots emerge. Phase of high risk of frost — a frost at this stage could destroy the entire harvest.

May–Jun
Flowering

The inflorescences open. Self-pollination is rapid (8–15 days). Temperature > 15 °C and absence of rain are essential — rain during flowering causes coulure (flower fall) and millerandage (poorly set berries).

Jul–Aug
Painter / Veraison

The berries change color (reds turn purple-black; whites turn yellow-green). Beginning of sugar accumulation and reduction in acidity. Marks the beginning of maturation.

Aug–Oct
Maturation and Harvest

Sugars accumulate, acidity decreases, varietal aromas develop. The harvest date is the most critical decision of the year.

Nov–Jan
Leaf Drop and Dormancy

Starch reserves migrate to the trunk and roots. The plant enters vegetative rest — the ideal phase for winter pruning.

Heat and Maturation

Grape maturation is a biochemical process that combines sugar accumulation, acid degradation, anthocyanin synthesis (color) and aroma development. Temperature is the main driver — and its sum throughout the season determines whether a variety reaches full maturation in a given region.

Accumulated Heat Hours

The Winkler index measures the sum of active temperatures above 10 °C throughout the season. It is the main indicator of the suitability of a region for a given variety.

What Defines the Harvest Date

The ideal harvest combines technological maturation (sugar content and acidity) with phenolic ripeness (ripe tannins and anthocyanins). Harvesting early preserves acidity but sacrifices body; late harvesting maximizes body and alcohol but risks oxidation and loss of freshness.

Phenolic Maturation vs. Technological

The two maturations do not always evolve in tandem. In very hot years, sugars spike before tannins ripen, creating wines with high alcohol but green tannins. In cool years, tannins ripen before sugars — harvesting early can result in balanced wines but with less body.

soil and Geology

soil is one of the pillars of Terroir — it influences the water regime, nutrient availability and, ultimately, the aromatic and structural profile of the wine. The vine thrives in poor, well-drained soils where scarcity of resources forces it to deepen its roots.

Types of soil

Sandy

  • Fast drainage, low water retention
  • Fast heating
  • Resistant to phylloxera (Colares)
  • Light, aromatic wines, low color

clay

  • High water and nutrient retention
  • Slow heating
  • Risk of waterlogging
  • Full-bodied wines, firm tannins

Shale (Slate)

  • Accumulates heat during the day, releases it at night
  • Low fertility, moderate water stress
  • Roots penetrate through cracks
  • Douro — concentration, minerality, spice

Granitic

  • Good drainage, good natural acidity
  • soils nutrient poor
  • Moderate water retention
  • Vinho Verde, Dão — high acidity, mineral freshness

Limestone

  • High pH, rich in calcium
  • Moderate to high water stress
  • Promotes acidity and elegance
  • Bairrada (clay-limestone) — freshness, tension, longevity

Basaltic/Volcanic

  • Rich in minerals, good drainage
  • soils young, very fertile
  • High salinity (Azores, Madeira)
  • Wines with distinct minerality and salinity

The Concept of Terroir

Terroir is the sum of all the natural factors of a place — soil, subsoil, topography, climate, microclimate — that give the wine a unique and irreproducible identity elsewhere. It is a fundamentally French concept but universally adopted.

Origin and Diversity of varieties

The vine belongs to the genus Vitis, with more than 60 species distributed throughout the northern hemisphere. Just Vitis vinifera — originating from the Caucasus — produces grapes of sufficient quality for wine. Domestication began between 6,000 and 8,000 BC, and the spread was the work of the Phoenicians, Greeks and Romans.

Natural and Human Selection

Over millennia, natural selection (adaptation to climate and local soil) and human selection (preference for certain characteristics) created enormous genetic diversity. Today there are more than 10,000 varieties identified worldwide, of which only a few hundred have relevant commercial expression.

Portugal is extraordinarily rich in indigenous varieties — it is estimated that there are more than 250 unique varieties, of which around 50 are productively relevant.

Ampelography

Ampelography is the science of identifying and describing varieties through their morphological characteristics — leaf, cluster, berry, shoot, flower. With DNA, it became possible to confirm kinship and uncover origins that morphology did not allow. Touriga Nacional, for example, was genetically confirmed as a close relative of Touriga Franca.

Portugal Wine

Portugal is one of the countries with the highest wine density in the world. With about 195,000 hectares of vineyards, is simultaneously one of the largest European producers and consumers of wine per capita, and the largest world exporter of Vinho Verde and Port wine.

Geographic and Climate Diversity

In a country of just 89,000 km², Portugal concentrates a remarkable climate variety: from the humid Atlantic climate of the Northwest to the dry continental Mediterranean of Alentejo, passing through the mountain continental of Beiras and the subtropical microclimates of Madeira and the Azores. This diversity, combined with the richness of indigenous varieties, is the main source of distinction for Portuguese wine.

Minho Driving Systems

The trellis (or ramada) is the traditional system of Minho: a high pergola that raises the vine above 1.5 m, allowing the cultivation of other crops underneath and protecting the clusters from the humidity of the soil. A modern espalier replaced the trellis on many farms, allowing mechanization and better vegetation management.

Wine Style

Vinhos Verdes whites are known for their freshness, high acidity and lightness (typically 8.5–11% alcohol). Natural perlage (residual CO₂) is characteristic. With the growth of Alvarinho, wines with more body, complexity and aging potential emerged, especially from Monção and Melgaço.

Wine Profile

The reds of Trás-os-Montes match rusticity and personality: ripe dark fruit and firm body in the warm years of Planalto Mirandês; elegance and freshness in reds of Chaves. The whites, based on Gouveio and Viosinho, surprise with its minerality and lively acidity, with a profile close to whites and Douro. The region, still underrepresented in the market, is a growing source of discovery for the curious oenophile.

Wine Profile

The whites of Tejo — especially those from Lezíria, base Fernão Pires — are fruity, floral and for young consumption: one of the most accessible and popular styles in Portugal. The reds, particularly those from Bairro (Castelão, Trincadeira), have a medium body, soft tannins and freshness that makes them versatile at the table. The region produces overwhelmingly under IGP (~90%), which provides varietal freedom and a good quality/price ratio. In recent decades, independent producers have asserted wines from Terroir with ambition age-worthy, changing the image of a merely volumetric region.

Colares — A unique case

The vines of Colares are planted in sand on clay, on a coastal strip swept by the Atlantic wind. The sand prevented the arrival of phylloxera — the insect cannot propagate in this substrate. Therefore, Colares vines are the rare European vines with their own roots, not grafted. variety Ramisco produces wines here with exceptional acidity and tannin, requiring age-worthy decades.

talha wines

The tradition of talha wines — fermented in large clay amphorae — has survived in Alentejo since Roman times. In 2010, it obtained its own DOC. Talha wines ferment and rest with the pomace (seeds, stems, skin) inside the talha, in prolonged contact with the clay. The result is wines with a unique texture, low sulfur content and controlled oxidative character.

Wine Profile

The reds from the Algarve vary from lightness to structure: the Negra Mole produces light wines, with fresh red fruit and soft tannins, ideal for young consumption; Castelão and Trincadeira allow more concentrated versions and aging potential. The whites — base Arinto and Síria — surprise with their freshness and natural acidity despite the intense heat. Algarve viticulture has modernized significantly since the 2000s: private producers have invested in varietal reconversion, indigenous varieties and quality over volume. The four DOCs (Lagos, Portimão, Lagoa, Tavira) cover the territory from west to east, but the majority of quality wine is sold as IGP Algarve, with greater creative freedom. International tourism has created a sustained local market receptive to experimentation.

The Process of estufagem heating and Canteiro

The Wine from Madeira It is unique for its controlled heating process — which mimics the conditions of long tropical sea voyages in the 18th and 19th centuries:

estufagem heating

  • Heating in stainless steel tanks with coil (~45 °C)
  • Duration: ~3 months
  • Used for Tinta Negra and commercial wines
  • Faster and more controlled process

Canteiro

  • Natural aging in barrels in sun-heated warehouses
  • Duration: years to decades
  • For Noble Varieties and higher quality wines
  • Slow oxidation, maximum complexity

Most winegrowers have plots of less than 0.3 ha and sell grapes to large winemaking companies: Blandy's, Henriques & Henriques, Barbeito, Justino's and D'Oliveiras. Climate and soils

The Azores have a humid temperate oceanic climate: mild temperatures, high precipitation, strong winds and air salinity. soils are predominantly basalts, with minimal cultivable layer — the vines grow planted directly in cracks in the lava rock.

The Curraletas — A Unique System in the World

The corraletas are small plots of vineyards delimited by loose basalt stone walls (dry stone walls), approximately 1 meter high. This ancestral system serves two functions:

  • Protection of the saline winds of the Atlantic
  • Temperature rise of soil by thermal irradiation of black basalt

Each corraleta is just a few square meters. The vines are planted directly in natural or open cracks in the rock. Everything is done manually — there is no mechanization possible in this system.

Styles and Denominations

The whites calm modern Azores have gained recognition for their minerality, salinity and high acidity — a unique profile generated by basalt, the sea and the island climate. These are wines of purity and sensorial originality that are increasingly valued by oenophiles.

Renovation and Wine Tourism

After a sharp decline in the 20th century, Azorean viticulture has been revitalized since 2000: replanting projects, conversion of corraletas and producers such as Azores Wine Company, Adega do Volcão and Corral Atlântis lead modernization. Wine tourism on the island of Pico, with visits to historic corraletas and tastings in old wineries, is a rapidly growing area.
