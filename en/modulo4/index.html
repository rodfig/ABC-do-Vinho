Alcoholic fermentation is the central process of winemaking: yeast converts the sugars present in must — essentially glucose and fructose — into ethanol and carbon dioxide, releasing heat energy in the process. It is an exothermic reaction that requires active control on the part of the winemaker. The typical sugar concentration in European musts is between 180–260 g/L (18–26°Brix), producing 11–15% potential alcohol.

The process develops in four distinct phases. A latency phase (lag phase) corresponds to the period of yeast adaptation to the environment, without visible cell growth. A exponential phase this is when the yeast population grows rapidly and alcohol production is maximum; the density of the wort drops to the naked eye. A deceleration phase occurs when the substrate begins to become scarce and ethanol begins to progressively inhibit metabolism. Finally, the stationary/final phase appears when the sugar is almost exhausted and the yeasts cease their fermentative activity.

FA Secondary Products

  • Glycerol (~7 g/L) — contributes to smoothness and body
  • Organic acids — succinic acid (bitter-salty taste), acetic acid (volatile acidity)
  • Higher alcohols — isoamyl, amyl, isobutyl; in excess they give solvent aromas
  • Esters — isoamyl acetate (banana), ethyl acetate (fruity); positive at low concentrations
  • Sulfur compounds — H₂S, mercaptans; unwanted, result from nutritional stress (nitrogen or elemental sulfur deficiency)

Enological Importance

  • Defines the alcohol content and therefore the structure and balance of the wine
  • Determines the secondary aromatic profile (esters, higher alcohols formed by yeast)
  • Ensures the microbiological stability of the final wine (ethanol is inhibitory)
  • Correct management of AF is the most determining factor in oenological quality
Rule of thumb

Potential alcohol (% v/v) ≈ 0.592 × (Brix − 3). A wort at 24°Brix produces approximately 12.4% alcohol. Every 17 g/L of sugar generates ~1% alcohol. Overripe grapes (>26 °Brix) increase stress on the yeast and the risk of failure.

The surface of the grapes is home to a diverse microbiota — yeasts, molds and bacteria — that varies depending on the variety, the region, the climate and the health of the harvest. This flora is the basis of spontaneous fermentations and provides part of the microbiological "Terroir" to the wine.

Factors that Influence the Natural Flora of Grapes

  • Precipitation and humidity — humid years favor fungi and bacteria; dry years favor yeast
  • Altitude — lower temperatures at altitude select distinct flora (more Kloeckera)
  • Phytosanitary treatments — fungicides drastically reduce epiphytic flora
  • Pest attacks (Botrytis, flies) — introduce unwanted microorganisms
  • Nitrogen fertilization — musts richer in nitrogen favor bacterial growth
  • Harvest waste management — pomace and stems left in the vineyard inoculate soil with diverse flora
  • Cellar asepsis conditions — clean and new wineries have less native flora; old cellars have established strains

Factors that Influence Yeast Survival

  • Temperature — outside 10–35 °C the activity ceases; above 40 °C are destroyed
  • Ethanol concentration — membrane toxicity above 13–14% vol.
  • Osmotic pressure — very concentrated musts (>30 °Brix) inhibit cell growth right from the beginning
  • pH — pH 3.0–3.4 favors yeast; lower or higher values are inhibitory
  • Nutrient availability — YAN <150 mg/L causes stress, H₂S production and risk of arrest

Cellar flora

Wineries with years of production accumulate stable populations of Saccharomyces cerevisiae on wood, pumps and surfaces — these “house” strains naturally lead to spontaneous fermentations. This is what is called "microbiological Terroir" and explains why the same winemaker in a different establishment obtains different wines.

The decision between inoculation with selected yeast and spontaneous fermentation is one of the first oenological choices of the harvest. Inoculation guarantees quick start, consistency and predictability of aromatic profile.

Characteristics of Good Wine Yeast

  • High ethanol tolerance (≥14% v/v)
  • Good fermentative capacity under stress conditions
  • Low production of volatile acidity (acetic acid)
  • Low production of sulfur compounds (H₂S)
  • Balanced production of higher alcohols and esters
  • Resistance to fermentation temperatures (12–30 °C)
  • Quick start and mastery over wild flora
  • Low foam production (ease of management)
  • Flocculation capacity (clean sedimentation)
  • Resistance to SO₂
  • Ability to ferment at low temperatures (whites)
  • Aromatic expression suited to the wine style
  • Absence of the killer factor (or carrier of the killer factor)

Active Dry Yeast (LSA)

  • Industrially dehydrated by freeze-drying or spray-drying process
  • Packed under nitrogen (N₂) atmosphere to protect from oxidation
  • Residual humidity ≤ 9% — guarantees viability and long conservation
  • Stored cold (4–8 °C); validity of 1 year after opening under correct conditions
  • Dehydration partially destroys cell membrane permeability
  • Mandatory rehydration in warm water 37–40°C, 20–30 minutes (Go-Ferm® recommended)
  • Doses: 0.1–0.3 g/L of must → approx. 10,000,000 cells/mL (10⁷ cells/mL)

Natural or Spontaneous Fermentation

In spontaneous fermentation, no yeast is added. The natural flora of the vineyard and winery guides the process. The result is generally more complex but less predictable.

AdvantagesRisks
Greater aromatic and microbiological Terroir complexitySlow start — longer contamination window
Diversity of wild strains contributes to unique profileRisk of stopping if the flora is not robust
No need for commercial yeast (cost and sustainability)Inconsistency between harvests — difficult to replicate
Valued in natural and organic winesIncreased risk of aromatic defects (H₂S, mercaptans, volatile acidity)

Wild strains: strains of Saccharomyces cerevisiae present in the winery for years, adapted to local conditions. They can be isolated, characterized and reused as mother cultures — a compromise between spontaneous and inoculated.

The Domain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae

  • Produces ethanol in concentrations lethal to competitors (exclusion effect)
  • Tolerates low pH, SO₂ and temperatures between 10–35 °C — more resistant than most non-Saccharomyces yeasts
  • Some strains produce killer toxin (K2) that eliminates sensitive yeast
  • In 4–5% alcohol, most non-Saccharomyces yeast die — S. cerevisiae takes complete control
factor killer

Some strains of Saccharomyces produce K2 toxin (killer factor), which kills other sensitive yeasts. In spontaneous fermentations, a dominant killer strain can suppress desirable strains. The solution: inoculate with a commercial strain that is also a killer — or carries the killer factor — to ensure dominance over the cellar's flora.

Carbon Metabolism

Glucose and fructose are catabolized by glycolysis to pyruvate. Depending on the glucose concentration and the availability of O₂, yeast chooses between respiration and fermentation:

Glucose ConcentrationMetabolic PathwayResult
< 0.2 g/LAerobic respiration (if O₂ available)CO₂ + H₂O + ATP (highest energy)

> 2 g/LFermentation (Crabtree Effect)Ethanol + CO₂ predominant even with O₂

Respiratory Substrates

In conditions of glucose shortage (<0.2 g/L), S. cerevisiae can breathe alternative compounds:

  • Pyruvate — product of glycolysis; oxidized in the Krebs cycle
  • Lactate — converted to pyruvate by lactate dehydrogenase
  • Acetate — oxidized via acetyl-CoA
  • Ethanol — can be reoxidized to acetaldehyde and then to acetate
  • Glycerol — converted to dihydroxyacetone phosphate and then oxidized

Limitation of nitrogen metabolism

Saccharomyces cerevisiae cannot metabolize nitrate (NO₃⁻), nitrite (NO₂⁻) or amines as a source of nitrogen. The only form of nitrogen assimilable for yeast are amino acids, ammonia (NH₄⁺) and certain sources of YAN (nitrogen assimilable by yeast). Therefore, nitrogen supplementation uses DAP (diammonium phosphate) and amino acid mixtures (Fermaid®), never nitrate.

Crabtree Effect

Even in the presence of oxygen, S. cerevisiae in musts rich in sugar (>2 g/L) it prefers the fermentative route. This phenomenon ensures that ethanol production starts immediately — a competitive advantage that yeast has evolved to exclude other microorganisms from the substrate. In almost exhausted musts (<0.2 g/L), respiration can be resumed if O₂ is present.

Glycolysis takes place in ten enzymatic reactions in the cytoplasm, converting one glucose molecule into two pyruvates. In the fermentative pathway, pyruvate is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde (CO₂ released) and then reduced to ethanol — regenerating NAD⁺ to keep the glycylytic flux active.

A fermentation stop is critical: the yeasts stop the sugar conversion before completion, leaving residual sugar that compromises the microbiological stability of the wine. Fermentation complete has a sigmoid curve, descending to ~0.995 g/cm³; the stop typically stagnates at around 1.030 g/cm³.

Toxic Effect of Ethanol

Accumulated ethanol is the main inhibitory agent at the end of fermentation. It acts through four mechanisms:

  1. Inhibits the absorption of sugars and amino acids — membrane transporters are compromised
  2. Damages phospholipid membranes — increases fluidity and non-selective permeability
  3. Causes loss of intracellular magnesium (Mg²⁺) and calcium (Ca²⁺) — essential for enzymes
  4. Reduces intracellular pH — compromises enzymatic activity and proton gradient

Production of Aromatic Defects by Fermentative Stress

  • Acetic acid + ethyl acetate — volatile acidity; aromas of vinegar and glue; result of nutritional stress or bacterial contamination
  • Higher alcohols (isoamyl, amyl) — in excess they give solvent aromas; produced in hot fermentation or with nitrogen stress
  • Volatile phenols (4-ethylphenol, 4-ethylguaiacol) — produced by Brettanomyces; aromas of barnyard and poultice
  • Sulfur compounds (H₂S, thiols, dimethyl sulfide, ethyl mercaptan) — result of nitrogen deficiency, residual elemental sulfur, or yeast autolysis

Intentional Stops

In certain styles, fermentation is deliberately stopped before completion, preserving residual sugar:

  • Late Harvest California Zinfandel — high concentration of sugar + high alcohol; fermentation to naturally
  • Amarone della Valpolicella — dried grapes (appassimento); yeasts stop by the combined stress of sugar and alcohol
  • Natural wines — without inoculation or nutrition; wild yeasts often stop before the end
  • Sauternes and other botrytises — very high sugar inhibits yeast
  • Vin Doux Naturel (France) — addition of brandy to stop fermentation (like Port)

Stop Resolution Protocol (6 Steps)

  1. Correct temperature — bring to 20–22 °C to reactivate metabolism
  2. Re-shake the lees (if the yeast is still viable) — bâtonnage can reactivate dormant cells
  3. Do not add vitamins without prior diagnosis — excess thiamine can be inhibitory
  4. Dead lees racking — remove lysed cells that release inhibitory fatty acids
  5. Lightly sulphite and re-inoculate with alcohol-tolerant killer strain — acclimatize progressively
  6. Correct nitrogen with DAP if YAN < 200 mg/L — essential nutrition for restart
restart protocol

1. Choose alcohol tolerant strain (≥14%). 2. Hydrate at 35–38°C with Go-Ferm®. 3. Adapt gradually: mix with equal parts of wine + fresh must → ferment for 4–6h → add increasing portions of still wine over 12–24h. 4. Monitor density daily. The key is the progressive acclimatization — ethanol shock is the main enemy of restart yeasts.

When fermentation is compromised — by nutritional stress, extreme temperatures, excess SO₂, elemental sulfur in the grapes or pesticide and herbicide residues — unwanted sulfur compounds appear. These compounds are highly volatile and detectable in minimal concentrations.

Defective mercaptans form in three distinct ways:

  1. Transformation of residual H₂S — reacts with carbonyl compounds in wine to form thioethers and mercaptans
  2. Autolysis of dead yeast — especially in prolonged aging on lees without bâtonnage; sulfur proteins degrade into mercaptans
  3. Chemical reduction in anoxic environment — oxidized sulfur compounds are reduced in the absence of O₂ to the reduced form (mercaptan)

Diagnosis Before Treatment

  • Perform a direct olfactory test to identify the type of defect (H₂S vs. mercaptan vs. varietal thiol)
  • Test with a drop of copper sulfate solution (0.5%) — if the defect temporarily disappears, the presence of H₂S or simple mercaptan is confirmed
  • If copper testing does not eliminate the defect, it may be longer chain thiols — consider controlled aeration or other methods

Prevention of Mercaptans

  • Ensure adequate YAN (≥200 mg/L) prior to fermentation — nitrogen deficiency is the most common cause
  • Avoid elemental sulfur (S⁰) in grapes — residues from late phytosanitary treatments
  • Carry out regular bâtonnage in aging on lees — avoid anoxic areas
  • Do not extend aging on grounds longer than necessary without periodic aeration
  • Timely transfers to avoid accumulation of dead cells in autolysis

Yeast Ethanol Tolerance

Yeast adapt to the increase in ethanol by: dysregulation of membrane permeability, formation of sterols and unsaturated fatty acids in the presence of O₂ (which is why aeration at the beginning is crucial), and increased production of the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase. Commercial yeasts are selected for 7 generations under stress conditions — natural yeasts require ~20 generations for the same adaptation.

Volume Changes During Fermentation

Sugar occupies more volume than the ethanol that results from it — hence the volume of must decreases throughout fermentation:

Total reduction
~44–50 mL per liter of wort (wort at 22°Brix): sugar (164 mL/L) converts to ethanol (114 mL/L) + CO₂ → ~50 mL/L contraction
Example at 22°Brix
Sugar: occupies 164 mL/L · Ethanol produced: 114 mL/L · Difference: −50 mL/L = 5% reduction in volume
Thermal expansion
Every 10°C above 20°C increases the volume by ~0.2% (liquid expansion); hot tanks appear fuller than they are
Practical implication
Never fill tanks to the limit before FA — leave room for foam and thermal expansion; correct the level after drying to avoid oxidation
A must at 24 °Brix

It has approximately 264 g/L of sugar. When completely fermented, it produces ~56 L of CO₂ per liter of wort. For 10,000 L of wort at 1°Brix/day: dVCO₂/dt = 55 × 11 / 210 × 10,000 = 28,800 L of CO₂ per day — a quantity that requires mandatory forced ventilation in the cellar.

Safety with CO₂

CO₂ is a gas denser than air — it accumulates in depressions, basements and confined spaces. In concentrations above 0.5% v/v it can cause asphyxiation. Necessary ventilation: outside air intake = 24.75× the volume of CO₂ generated. For 10,000 L of wort at 1 °Brix/day: need for ≈ 713,000 L of fresh air per day.

Advantages of FML

  • Reduction of total acidity — malic acid (dicarboxylic) converted to lactic acid (monocarboxylic); less feeling of hardness
  • Increased aromatic complexity — diacetyl (FML product) gives buttery and creamy notes; positive in reds and certain whites (Chardonnay)
  • Microbiological stability — consumption of malic acid deprives bacteria of substrate; reduces risk of spontaneous FML in the bottle
  • Increased volume and greasiness — mannoproteins released by bacteria enrich the texture

Disadvantages (If Uncontrolled)

  • Production of excess volatile acidity — contaminating bacteria produce acetic acid
  • Production of unwanted phenols — caffeic and coumaric acid derivatives
  • Loss of freshness and primary aromas in aromatic whites (Alvarinho, Moscatel, Viosinho)
  • Formation of biogenic amines — histamine, tyramine — health risk if in high concentrations
  • FML in bottle — unwanted CO₂, turbidity and volatile acidity

Behavior of Anthocyanins in Wine

Chemical explanation (simplified): at low (acidic) pH, the structure flavylium predominates — stable and intensely red. At higher pH, the molecules convert to less conjugated forms (carbinol, quinonoid, chalcone) with less absorption of visible light, which changes the color to violet, blue or even colorless. This behavior is reversible within certain limits, which allows anthocyanins to be used as natural pH indicators.

  • red wines are normally between pH 3.2 and 3.6 — the dominant color is red to purple
  • If the pH rises (due to poor winemaking or environmental factors), the color gets lost quickly and bluish-gray tones appear
  • The blue color in wines is not desirable — associated with instability and oxidation
  • In rosés, the addition of SO₂ at bottling may cause temporary loss of color (reversible discoloration), which returns to normal after 2–3 days

Fick's Law of Maceration

Q = K × S × L × (C − c) / h

Q
Amount of solute extracted per unit of time
K
Diffusion coefficient (depends on temperature and nature of the solute)
S
Contact surface (solid-liquid area)
L
Degree of leaching (ability to release the compound from the solid)
C − c
Concentration gradient between skin and must
h
Diffusion layer thickness

This formula shows why agitation, temperature, increased contact surface and renewal of the liquid next to the solid blanket increase extraction efficiency. Contact methods (reassembly) influence the rate of extraction, but do not change the total quantity extracted — which depends on the balance.

Role of Tannins in the Extraction of Anthocyanins

The oenological tannins added to the must create a 3-step mechanism that amplifies the extraction of anthocyanins:

  1. Kidnapping — exogenous tannins react with free anthocyanins in the must, forming stable pigment-tannin complexes (the anthocyanin is "captured")
  2. Breaking the balance — removal of free anthocyanins from wort disrupts the extraction balance between skin and wort
  3. New gradient — with fewer free anthocyanins in the must, a new concentration gradient is created (C−c in Fick's Law) that forces additional extraction of skins
Anthocyanins and tannins: extraction dynamics

Anthocyanins reach a maximum after 2–3 days of maceration — then decrease through degradation and polymerization with tannins. Tannins, on the contrary, continue to increase throughout maceration. Adding commercial oenological tannins to must reacts with free anthocyanins, forms stable pigment-tannin complexes, and "shifts the balance" allowing additional extraction of anthocyanins from skin.

During the fermentation of reds, the skins, seeds and stems rise to the surface forming the "hat" (solid blanket). Pumping over and delestage are the techniques to manage this hat — extracting compounds and homogenizing the must.

Purposes of Reassembly

  • Reoxygenate the wort — supply O₂ to yeast in exponential phase, essential for the biosynthesis of sterols and unsaturated fatty acids
  • Homogenize temperature and yeast — the hat is warmer; pumping over balances thermal gradients and distributes yeast throughout the must
  • Increase extraction of color, aroma and tannins — the renewed contact between must and skin reestablishes the gradient (Fick's Law) and intensifies extraction
  • Cool the blanket — the solid blanket reaches higher temperatures that can kill yeast and produce defects; wetting it regularly reduces this risk
  • Facilitate liquid/blanket contact — dry blanket creates anoxic zones suitable for acetic bacteria; reassembly prevents this problem

The Delestage Process

  1. Completely empty the fermentative wort into an auxiliary tank, leaving skins's hat in the background of the vat.
  2. The must is reintroduced from above with force, breaking the hat and completely wetting the solid parts.
  3. It can be repeated 1 to 2 times a day, especially at the beginning of fermentation.

Advantage over continuous reassembly: Better extraction of color, tannins and aromatic compounds; controlled oxygenation; reduction of green/astringent tannins (more delicate because the intense contact is brief).

Wort Hyperoxygenation

Technique applied to white musts and rosés before AF. It consists of the deliberate and controlled addition of O₂, promoting the early oxidation of unstable phenolic compounds (catechins, flavonoids), which precipitate and are removed by Decanting — before passing into the final wine.

Chemical Reaction of Hyperoxygenation

The enzyme polyphenol oxidase (PPO), present in white musts, catalyzes the oxidation of unstable phenolic compounds in the presence of O₂:

  1. PPO oxidizes catechins, flavonoids and other phenolics → quinones (brown compounds)
  2. Quinones polymerize and precipitate (insoluble in must) → brown particles are formed
  3. Decanting or filtration removes these precipitates before fermentation — the wort is protected from further oxidation
Objectives
Remove oxidized phenolics; stabilize color (delay yellowing); reduce future sensitivity to oxidation; increase aromatic complexity
Suitable for
Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Trebbiano, Airén, Chenin Blanc — whites rich in phenolics sensitive to oxidation
Contraindicated in
Aromatic grapes (Moscatel, Gewürztraminer) — oxidizes terpenoids that define the varietal aroma; may reduce primary fruity aromas
Discovery
Developed in the 1970s in Germany and France by winemaker Kurt Düring, to resolve late oxidation in whites

Cryo-extraction

It consists of cool whole or de-stemmed grapes to temperatures close to 0 °C (or −1 to −3 °C) before pressing, partially disrupting the cell walls of skin. This facilitates the release of aromatic precursors while the low temperature prevents the solubilization of anthocyanins.

Cautions and Limitations of Cryo-extraction

  • High energy cost — maintaining negative temperatures requires intense refrigeration, especially in hot regions
  • Risk of oxidation without inert gases — at low temperatures, enzymatic reactions are slow but oxidation is possible; use N₂ or CO₂ atmosphere during cooling
  • Strict hygiene control — the refrigerated environment favors the development of fungi and bacteria if cleaning is poor; disinfection of equipment is critical
Blanc de Noirs

A Blanc de Noirs is a white wine produced from In reds grapes — typically Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier in Champagne. Cryo-extraction followed by gentle pressing ensures that the must is clear, without anthocyanins. The result has more structure and aromatic complexity than a Blanc de Blancs (grapes whites), with greater potential for longevity.

Pétillants naturels (pét-nats)

Natural wines bottled with some dissolved CO₂ and live fermentation residue. The second fermentation takes place directly in the bottle, without draft liquor, producing light and irregular effervescence. Closed with crown capsule (not cork). Style increasingly popular in Portugal — Bairrada, Vinho Verde, Dão.

Classic Process — Steps

  1. Pressing of entire cluster: must without phenolics; Champagne legal limit = 102 L per 160 kg of grapes
  2. Primary fermentation: base wine ~9% alc., high acidity; in Champagne we look for secondary (non-tropical) aromas
  3. Circulation: addition of the tirage liqueur (sugar + yeast) before bottling with a temporary cap
  4. 2nd fermentation in the bottle: 4–8 weeks; 24 g/L sugar → 5–6 atm CO₂ + 1.2–1.3% alc.
  5. Yeast autolysis (sur lie): minimum 15 months (mostly Champagne), 36 months (Vintage); provides bread, brioche, dried fruits
  6. Remuage: progressive rotation of the bottles to move dregs to the neck
  7. Disgorgement: expulsion of dregs by freezing the neck + internal pressure
  8. Expedition liquor: adjustment of final sweetness; followed by corking with caging

Portuguese sparkling wines

Portugal has a sparkling tradition, especially in Bairrada (classic method with Bical and Arinto) and in the Douro. Denominations of Origin require at least 9 months of aging sur lie. The natural acidity of Portuguese varieties — Arinto, Síria, Fernão Pires — is a competitive advantage for fresh, elegant sparkling wines with good longevity.

Fraud detection

Genuine Port Wine can be distinguished by analyzing the pyruvic acid. A complete fermentation consumes all the pyruvate. In Porto, fermentation is stopped before the end — that's why there is residual pyruvic acid. Its absence in a supposed "Porto" is an indicator of fraud. Anthocyanins (malvidin-3-O-glucoside predominant in Vitis vinifera) also serve as markers of authenticity.

Wines from Curtimenta (also called "orange" or "amber") are whites vinified with prolonged skin maceration — exactly as if they were reds. Ancient technique with origins in the Caucasus, present in Portugal since Antiquity (wine from talha Alentejo).

Alentejo talha

The wine from talha is an ancient tradition from Alentejo: white and red grapes ferment together directly in large clay amphorae. talha "breathes" through the porous clay without chemically interfering. By law, wine from Alentejo talha can only be uncovered from Saint Martin's Day, November 11th. It is served directly from talha in restaurants in the region.
