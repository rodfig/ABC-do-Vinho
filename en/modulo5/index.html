What is a Espumante?

A Espumante is a wine that contains dissolved carbon dioxide (CO₂), which manifests itself in the form of effervescent bubbles when opened. This gas is produced naturally during alcoholic fermentation and trapped inside the bottle or tank, depending on the chosen production method.

The charm of sparkling wines lies in the vivacity of the bubbles, which add freshness, texture and complexity to the wine. The quality of the bubbles — size, persistence and Perlage — is a direct indicator of the quality of the Espumante: fine and persistent bubbles are synonymous with slow production and prolonged autolysis on the yeast lees.

Perlage
The set of bubbles visible in the glass — fine and numerous bubbles indicate good quality; thick and fast bubbles reveal industrial production
Pressure
Large sparklers reach 5–6 atm; Frizzante (slightly effervescent) is between 1–2.5 atm
Final sweetness
Determined by expedition liquor added after disgorgement — from Extra Brut (<6 g/L) to Doux (>50 g/L)
Why second fermentation?

The first fermentation produces a dry, non-effervescent base wine. It is the second fermentation — induced by the addition of sugar and yeast (tirage liquor) — that generates the CO₂ that, trapped, makes the wine Espumante. The equation is the same as for alcoholic fermentation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂.

The traditional method — method Champenoise when applied to Champagne — it is the most prestigious process for producing sparkling wines. The second fermentation takes place directly in the bottle, where the CO₂ is trapped and the yeast lees remain in contact with the wine for months or years, imparting complexity and unique autolytic aromas.

In addition to the traditional method, there are other processes to produce sparkling wine, each with different sensory characteristics, cost and level of complexity:

Transfer Method
Identical to traditional until autolysis. The bottles are then opened and the wine transferred to a tank, filtered, dosed and rebottled under pressure. Preserves part of the autolytic complexity with industrial efficiency
Tank Method (Charmat)
The second fermentation takes place in a pressurized tank. When the pressure reaches 5 atm, the wine is cooled to -5 °C, stabilized, dosed and bottled. Result: fruitier, younger wines with less autolytic complexity. Prosecco is the most famous example
Ancestral Method (Pét-Nat)
The fermentation of the must begins in the tank and ends in the bottle, without adding sugar or yeast. The CO₂ from the original fermentation is naturally trapped. It is often unfiltered — turbulent, rustic wines with great variation between bottles
Artificial Carbonation
CO₂ is added directly to wine, as in soft drinks. Larger and less persistent bubbles, without complexity. Inexpensive method for starter wines
Charmat vs. Traditional

Charmat favors the freshness and primary aromas of variety — ideal for Prosecco with variety Glera. The traditional method sacrifices young fruit in favor of autolytic complexity developed during sur lie. The choice should correspond to the desired style, not the cost.

Port Wine is one of the most emblematic expressions of Portuguese winemaking, with deep historical roots and a unique complexity resulting from the combination of geographic, human and technical factors. This fortified wine, known worldwide for its sweetness and intensity, is produced exclusively in the demarcated region of Douro — the oldest regulated wine region in the world.

The development of Porto is intrinsically linked to commercial relations between Portugal and England. In the 17th century, English imports of French wines were often banned or highly taxed, leading British merchants to look for alternatives in Portugal. The wines available at the time were generally thin and astringent or extremely dark and hard, known as blackstrap.

To improve stability during maritime transport, wine spirits were added before boarding. The real revolution occurred when a merchant discovered in Lamego, in Douro, a monastery where monks added brandy during fermentation — interrupting it and preserving the natural sugars of the grapes.

Methuen Treaty (1703)
It gave tax advantages to Portuguese wines in England, boosting Porto’s trade
Scandals of 1730
Adulteration with sugar and elderberry juice led to the creation of regulations and the official delimitation of the region by the Marquês de Pombal

The Douro region extends from approximately 65 km upstream from the city of Porto to the border with Spain. The mountains that surround the region protect it from Atlantic influence, creating a unique interior microclimate — dry, hot summers, harsh winters and precipitation concentrated in the coldest months.

soil
Dominant shale, surrounded by granite. Vineyards planted on terraces dug into steep slopes, with soils poor, stony and shallow (1–2 m) — which force the roots to descend in depth, concentrating the wines
Climate
Progressively more extreme from west to east — from Porto (1,200 mm rain, 35 °C summer) to the Spanish border (400 mm, >35 °C, with hail and prolonged droughts)
Aging
Traditionally in Vila Nova de Gaia, on the edge of the Atlantic, where the cooler and more humid climate prevents "Douro Bake" — oxidation caramelized by the extreme heat of the interior
Dams
The construction of dams in the 1960s transformed the river into large lakes, facilitating navigation and irrigation of young vineyards

The Douro region is divided into three sub-regions with distinct climatic, geological and qualitative characteristics:

Baixo Corgo
The westernmost and wettest part, centered around the city of Régua. It produces lighter wines, the main origin being Ruby and Tawnys of lower quality and more affordable cost
Cima Corgo
It is the heart of the region, centered on Pinhão. With less rain and higher temperatures, it is where the best wines are produced — Vintage, LBV and aged Tawnys. All major exporters have properties in this sub-region
Douro Superior
The easternmost, remote and least developed area. The terrain is flatter and the climate is even warmer. The increase in labor costs has led producers to invest here due to the greater ease of mechanization

Viticulture in Douro is complex and adapted to the region's challenging conditions. The old ones terraces they were narrow, with just two rows of vines supported by stone walls; the modern wider terraces reach densities of around 6,000 vines/ha. The landings, introduced in the 1970s, replace walls with embankments covered by vegetation — allowing mechanization, but with lower density (~3,500 vines/ha). The vineyards at the top They are planted vertically on slopes greater than 30°, making access difficult and worsening erosion.

The harvest generally starts at the end of September and lasts around three weeks. yields is very low — 500–750 kg/ha in old vines. You boars They are notorious pests in the region, as they eat the grapes in the weeks before harvest.

Classification A–F
Vineyards are classified based on 12 criteria (altitude, exposure, slope, varieties, age, etc.). The maximum score is 2,031 points — properties with more than 1,200 receive an A; below 200 receive F
Benefit
The maximum quantity of Port wine that each vineyard can produce annually, determined by IVDP based on classification. Vineyards A or B produce around 500–600 liters of Port per thousand vines
Fragmentation
More than 80% of the plots are less than 5 hectares — an extremely fragmented structure that makes mechanization difficult but guarantees diversity
Pruning
Old vines pruned in Guyot with wires; the most recent in spur with VSP driving. Irrigation is crucial for young vines

The challenge in producing Port Wine red is to quickly extract the phenolic compounds (color and tannins) before the alcohol content reaches 5–6%, when the brandy is added, stopping fermentation and preserving the natural sugars.

Treading on Foot (Lagares)
Traditional method in granite mills — gentle, effective and avoids crushing the seeds. Each mill has a capacity of 15 barrels (5,500–8,250 liters), requiring 1–2 people per Pipa. Still used on more traditional farms and for higher quality Ports
Autovinifiers
Modern system that uses the pressure of CO₂ produced during fermentation to pump the must over the solids blanket — the mechanical equivalent of treading. Some homes use robots that imitate human movement
Aguardentação (Mutagem)
When the must reaches 6–8% alcohol and retains 90–100 g/L of residual sugar, wine spirit of 77% ABV is added in a 1:4 ratio. The result is a wine of around 20% ABV

Wines intended for aging are traditionally transported to Vila Nova de Gaia, where the cooler and wetter climate, close to the Atlantic, prevents "Douro Bake" — the oxidative and caramelized aging caused by the extreme heat inside. In Gaia, the wines maintain more balanced color, freshness and complexity.

Cask Aged (aged at Madeira)
Wine remains at Pipa during the aging period, with gradual exposure to oxygen. Ready to drink after bottling — often filtered for clarity. Includes Ruby basic, Tawny and variants
Bottle Aged
Short aging to Madeira and unfiltered bottling. It evolves in the bottle for decades, developing a complex bouquet and deposit. Includes LBV Traditional and Vintage Port
Oak Kites
Port wines are aged in 550–630 liter oak barrels (or "vessels"). The intermediate size — larger than a Burgundy barrique, smaller than a large barrel — allows slow and controlled exchanges with oxygen

The wines of Madeira are one of the most unique and lasting expressions of world oenology, produced on the island of the same name — an autonomous region of Portugal located around 1,000 km from the continent and 750 km from the north coast of Africa.

Madeira was discovered in the 14th century, initially producing unfortified wines. Over time, Funchal became a natural port of call for vessels heading to Africa, Asia and South America. However, the wines were unstable during long ocean voyages. Around the 18th century, the practice of fortifying wines with alcohol from local sugar cane emerged.

During tropical crossings, it was observed that wine improved after the continuous movement of ships and the heat of the equatorial zones. Two aging methods have been established: Roda Wine (which matured during the trip) and the Wine Canteiro (which aged slowly in Funchal warehouses). With the decline of long sea voyages around 1900, the artificial system of greenhouse.

The 6 Current Exporters

The Madeira wine industry has faced serious adversities over the centuries, which have profoundly shaped its current structure:

Diseases (19th century)
Powdery mildew in 1851, followed by phylloxera, devastated the vineyards. As a quick solution, hybrids and American vines were introduced — initially as rootstocks, but later also for direct wine production. This caused a significant drop in the region's quality reputation
Russian Revolution (1917)
It cut off important markets — Russia was one of the main destinations for the sweetest Madeiras
Prohibition in the US (1920s–30s)
It eliminated a significant portion of sales. The Americans, who in the 1790s purchased around 25% of production (even toasting the Declaration of Independence with wine Madeira), stopped importing
Mergers and Concentration
Many companies closed; others merged into the Madeira Wine Company (today part of the Blandy's group). Today only six exporters remain active

Madeira has a subtropical, hot and humid climate, with abundant rainfall — up to 3,000 mm annually in higher altitude areas. This favors the emergence of diseases such as gray rot (Botrytis) and powdery mildew. The vines are planted in small plots on narrow terraces, inaccessible to machines, requiring intensive manual work. The altitude and the presence of clouds in high areas contribute to the preservation of acidity in the grapes.

soil
Volcanic, fertile, formed by decomposing red and yellow tuffs and small fragments of basalt rich in iron. The island rises steeply to 1,800 meters, creating varied microclimates
Levadas
Traditional irrigation canals that distribute water from high, humid areas to dry slopes — a centuries-old water engineering system
Elevated Trusses
To reduce the risk of fungi, vines are often grown on raised trellises (lattices), keeping the foliage away from damp soil
Microclimates
The diversity of altitude (0–1,800 m), exposure and precipitation creates microclimates that determine which variety is best suited to each location

The aging process is the most unique and defining element of Madeira wines. Controlled exposure to heat — whether artificially (greenhouse) or natural (Canteiro) — causes a series of chemical reactions that make this wine practically indestructible and with unparalleled longevity in the wine world:

Greenhouse — Heat Tank
The wine is heated between 40–50 °C for at least 3 months, in stainless steel or concrete tanks (20,000–50,000 l), with hot water coils. It is the most common method for the most accessible styles and is regulated by the Wine Institute of Madeira
Greenhouse — Heat Warehouse
Smoother process, with heating at 30–40 °C for 6 to 12 months, in Madeira 600 l barrels. Uses heat from hot water pipes. Applied to some Reserve and Special Reserve category wines
Canteiro
Traditional and most prestigious method. The wine matures slowly in 600 l barrels heated by the natural heat of the sun in Funchal warehouses — where temperatures can exceed 45 °C in summer. It can age for 20 to 100 years or more. Exclusive to Madeiras Vintage (Frasqueira)
Maderization vs. Oxidation

The heat from the oven causes Maillard and caramelization reactions that create the Madeira's characteristic aromas of caramel, coffee, nuts and spices. Unlike other wines, where oxidation is a defect, in Madeira it is an intrinsic part of the style — hence its exceptional longevity and resistance to bottle aging.

The wines of Jerez — internationally known as Sherry — are the result of a long winemaking tradition that dates back more than three millennia. The city of Jerez de la Frontera, in southern Spain, is considered one of the oldest wine-growing towns in the country. The introduction of viticulture occurred in the 12th century BC, around 1100 BC, with the Phoenicians.

By the end of the 15th century, the Jerez wine trade with England and France was already solid. In 1492, many vineyards were taken over by the English — establishing at that time the size of the "Sherry Butt", the oak still used in maturation. Francis Drake's attacks on Jerez rekindled British interest in the local wine, which came to be known as "sack".

Sanlúcar de Barrameda played a strategic role in the 16th century as a port of departure for the Americas — Jerez's wines fueled fleets sailing to the New World, establishing the region's global reputation long before the industrial era. At the beginning of the 19th century, figures such as Pedro Domecq and Manuel María González Ángel (founder of González Byass) were fundamental in structuring modern industry. However, the plague phylloxera, at the end of the 19th century, devastated the region's vineyards, forcing replantings on American rootstocks and drastically reducing the production area.

Sherry Shippers Association (1910)
Created to organize the industry after the disorderly expansion of the 20th century, which generated falling prices and drastic variations in quality
VOS and VORS (2000)
Quality labels introduced in 2000: VOS (Very Old Sherry, average of 20 years) and VORS (Very Rare Old Sherry, more than 30 years). Applicable to Oloroso, Palo Cortado, Amontillado and Pedro Ximénez
20th Century Decline
The total area of vineyards has fallen from more than 20,000 ha in the 1970s to the current 10,350 ha, with many vines replaced by sunflowers and cereals

DO Jerez is located in the province of Cádiz and covers three producing cities: Jerez de la Frontera (winemaking), Sanlúcar de Barrameda and El Puerto de Santa María (where the aging wineries are located). The soils play a central role in the identity of the wines:

The harvest traditionally begins on September 8th and lasts about a month. The grapes are de-stemmed and pressed in horizontal pneumatic presses. The tear must is vinified separately — ideal for Finos. Fermentation takes place at temperatures of up to 28 °C, avoiding esters and fruity aromas. The wines reach 12–13% alcohol, and the flower begins to develop naturally.

Double Classification
The wines are classified twice — in January and in the spring. Light soils wines tend to become Fino; those of soils heavy, Oloroso
Fortification
Finos are fortified to 15.5% with mitad and mitad (mixture of 95.5% alcohol and young wine). Olorosos at 17.5%. The difference in graduation determines whether the flower survives or not
Solera System
Used American oak hulls, arranged in floors (criaderas). The wine is taken from Solera (lower floor) and refilled with younger wine from the upper cáderas. Never remove more than a third of any hull at one time
Boots
oak of 600–650 liter American oak, intentionally filled to just five-sixths of capacity — the empty space allows controlled contact with the oxygen necessary for flower development

The flower is a veil of yeast of the type Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. beticus (also called Saccharomyces ellipsoid) that develops naturally on the surface of certain wines, especially in the Fino and Manzanilla styles. This biofilm is one of the most distinctive characteristics of biological aging, protecting the wine from oxidation and profoundly modifying its sensory characteristics.

The flower consumes oxygen, ethanol, glycerol and other nutrients from the wine, releasing compounds such as acetaldehyde — responsible for the characteristic aromas of green apple, bread dough and nuts. On the other hand, this metabolism prevents direct oxidation, keeping the wine fresh, dry and light.

Conditions for the Flower
It appears spontaneously in wines that reach around 15% ABV — a concentration that inhibits undesirable microorganisms but still allows the survival of these specialized yeasts. Above 16%, alcohol becomes toxic to the flower
Influence of Climate
In Montilla-Moriles, extreme summer heat kills the flower seasonally. In Sanlúcar de Barrameda, the cooler, wetter climate allows the flower to grow thick and even throughout the year, resulting in Manzanilla — the most delicate of the Sherries
Solera and Flower
The breeding system and Solera is essential: by being constantly fed with young wines rich in nutrients, the flower remains alive for 6 to 8 years or more, allowing for continuous and balanced aging
Manzanilla — The Flower of Sanlúcar

Manzanilla is produced exclusively in Sanlúcar de Barrameda, where the cooler and humid coastal climate favors a thicker and more stable flower throughout the year. The result is a Sherry with a unique character — saline, delicate, with notes of chamomile and a floral touch — that many consider the most refined expression of biological aging in Jerez.

Fino
Dry, light, protected from oxidation by the flower. Filtered and fortified to 15.5%. In Sanlúcar it is called Manzanilla; when aged for more than 8 years, it becomes Manzanilla Pasada
Amontillado
Starts as Fino for at least 3 years. It is then transferred, refortified, and begins to age oxidatively. It may be sweetened or contain younger wines. Fino Amontillado and Manzanilla Pasada indicate intermediate aging
Oloroso
It does not pass for flower, as it is fortified to 17.5–18% from the beginning. It ages oxidatively, reaching up to 24% alcohol. Sweet versions (Cream) are made with PX or Palomino mistela

Rare style that starts under flower like Fino, but this is inhibited before consuming all the glycerol. It then ages oxidatively, combining the structure of Oloroso with the floral aromas of Fino

Located 40 km south of Córdoba, Montilla-Moriles became a DO in 1945. Its wines can be fortified or not, and mostly use Pedro Ximénez (70%). Despite exporting part of the must to Jerez, it faces a reputation for being inferior to Sherry.

With a semi-arid and continental climate, scorching summers (up to 45 °C) and predominantly sandy soils, Montilla produces unfortified Finos — at Pedro Ximénez naturally reaches 15% ABV through complete fermentation — and fortified or unfortified oxidative styles.

Without Fortif. in Finos
Unlike Jerez, Finos de Montilla-Moriles does not need to be fortified. At Pedro Ximénez, due to the hot climate, it naturally reaches 15% alcohol — a level sufficient to sustain flower development
Flower in Montilla
The layer of flor that forms is significantly thinner than that found in Jerez, which results in wines that are more delicate, dry and oxidized more quickly. In summer, the flower tends to die in extreme heat.
Nomenclature
Within Spain, the use of the terms "Fino", "Oloroso" and the like is permitted, but this nomenclature cannot be used for export — the wines are labeled according to their sensory profile: Pale Dry, Medium Dry, Medium Sweet, Pale Cream, Cream
Tinajas
Traditional cement or clay containers (giant amphorae) used before moving to the Solera system — a singularity of Montilla

Produced in the Peloponnese from the red Mavrodaphne grape, this Greek fortified wine undergoes long aging in barrels, resulting in a style similar to Tawny. Aromatic and powerful, it presents notes of dried fruits, caramel and sweet spices
Commandaria (Cyprus)
Traditional fortified wine from Cyprus, produced in the demarcated region around the slopes of the Troodos Mountains. Grapes: red Mavro and white Xynisteri, harvested ripe and left in the sun to concentrate sugars. Fermentation stopped naturally; fortified wine up to 20% ABV. Minimum maturity of two years in barriques
Cyprus Fortified Wine (formerly "Cyprus Sherry")
For years marketed as "Cyprus Sherry", it was produced using methods similar to Port Wine: fermentation interrupted with alcohol, followed by aging for around a year at barriques, sweetened with concentrated must (RCGM). Some producers created dry versions aged under flower at Solera for at least two years
The End of "Cyprus Sherry"

With Cyprus' accession to the European Union in 2004, the name "Sherry" became protected exclusively for wines from the Jerez region in Spain. Cypriot producers were forced to rename their products — one of several designation of origin disputes resolved with European integration and the protection of geographical indications.
