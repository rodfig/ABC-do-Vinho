Introduction: The Role of Terroir

The quality and style of a wine are fundamentally shaped by the concept of Terroir, a French term that designates the set of natural and human factors that influence the profile of the grape and, consequently, the wine. Terroir It is not just soil, but a complex interaction between climate, microclimate, relief, type of soil, orientation of the vines, cultural practices and human traditions.

Climate and Microclimate

Climate is one of the central elements of Terroir. It determines the rhythm of the vegetative cycle, the ripeness of the grapes and the balance between acidity and sugar. O microclimate, in turn, refers to the specific conditions of a small area, and may even be delimited by a slope, plot or individual vineyard. Even more localized is the so-called micro-microclimate, influenced by plant barriers, proximity to bodies of water, walls, among others.

Climate change and its effects:

  • Water and Irrigation: Water deficit or excess affects the concentration of sugars and phenolic compounds.
  • Sun Exposure and Temperature: Optimal exposure allows for efficient photosynthesis. Higher altitudes moderate the heat and increase the temperature range, favoring acidity. Lower altitudes tend to produce more alcoholic and mature wines.
  • Vineyard orientation: Slopes facing south (in the northern hemisphere) receive more sunlight, promoting greater maturation.
  • Rivers and lakes they regulate the surrounding temperature through reflection and evaporation, influencing the microclimate of nearby vineyards.

Soil

The soil provides the vine with support, water and nutrients. Its physical and chemical composition affects root development, nutrient absorption and water regulation. Among the main types of soil:

  • Limestone: Wines with good acidity, minerals.
  • Shale: Rich wines, with spicy notes.
  • Granite: Minerals and spices.
  • Clay: They tend to produce warm wines, with ripe fruit and lower acidity.
  • Volcanic: Powerful, mineral wines with spices.

The temperature of the soil, its microbial fauna and water resources directly influence the vigor and depth of the roots. Controlling soil humidity and temperature is also essential to avoid diseases and keep the vine healthy.

Human Interaction

Cultural tradition, viticulture practices and the producer's philosophy have a profound impact on wine. From choosing the variety to the winemaking method, the human factor closes the cycle of Terroir.

Climatic Profiles of Portuguese Wines

Portugal presents a great diversity of wine styles influenced by climate, relief and soil.

Atlantic Wines (North and Coast)

Ex.: Vinhos Verdes, Bairrada, Lisboa, Azores, Madeira

  • Cool and humid climate, with strong oceanic influence
  • Long vegetative cycle
  • Low to medium alcohol content
  • High acidity
  • Light to medium body
  • Floral and fresh aromas preserved by the climate

Mountain Wines (Interior North)

Ex.: Douro, Dão, Trás-os-Montes, Beira Interior, North of Alentejo

  • Continental climate, altitude moderating the heat
  • Long vegetative cycle
  • Medium to high alcohol content
  • Medium to high acidity
  • Medium to full body
  • Greater aging potential

Plain Wines (Center and South)

Ex.: Tejo, Setúbal, Alentejo Center and South, Algarve

  • Hot, dry climate with a lower temperature range
  • Short vegetative cycle
  • Medium to high alcohol content
  • More tender acidity
  • Light to medium body, less structure, sometimes lean wines

Impact of Harvest and Climate on Sensory Profile

Hot Year

  • Rapid maturation, late harvest with risk of overripeness
  • Aromas of fig, raisin and jam
  • Hot, sweet, alcoholic mouth, with risk of imbalance

Cold Year

  • Incomplete ripeness, risk of green notes
  • Aromas of green peppers, fresh herbs, spinach
  • High acidity, astringent and bitter tannins
  • Balance needed with fruit and flowers to please

Rotten Year

  • Rotten, "moldy" grapes, no chance of satisfactory wine
  • Notes of dirty, dusty, rotten decomposition

Big Year

  • Perfect fruit at maturity, without mold or vegetable characteristics
  • Aromas rich in fruits, flowers, aromatic herbs
  • Evolution towards aromas of forest, spices, jam, animal notes

Visual Analysis

White Wines

  • Colorless with greenish reflections: Very young, protected from oxidation
  • Light yellow with greenish reflections: Young, fermented in vat
  • Straw yellow/gold: Mature, possibly fermented in wood
  • Coppery/tanned gold: Advanced aging
  • Amber: Evident oxidation, old wine

Classification by body (whites)

Not very full-bodied: Yellow to pale green, bright silvery. Greater acidity. Ex: Pinot Grigio, Albariño, Muscadet
Full-bodied: Bright platinum pale gold. Moderate acidity. Ex: Sauvignon Blanc, Trebbiano, Chenin Blanc
Very full-bodied: Bright bronze yellow ocher. Low acidity, smooth flavors. Ex: Chardonnay, Viognier, Semillon

Age of white wine

New Wines: Saturated color, yellow to green glow. High acidity and fresh flavors.
Aged Wines: Dull color, more faded, yellow and brown. Acidity decreases, tertiary nutty notes appear.

Red Wines

  • Ruby with violet halo: Young wine
  • Pure red/ruby: Some evolution
  • Ruby with brownish reflections: Aging in wood, beginning of aging (3 to 7 years)
  • Brownish red to brown: Apogee or decline depending on quality
  • Amber: Oxidized, usually excessive aging

Classification by body (reds)

Not very full-bodied: Wide, transparent ring. Few tannins, high acidity. Ex: Pinot Noir, Gamay
Full-bodied: Medium ring, semi-transparent, opaque interior. Moderate tannins, medium acidity. Ex: Tempranillo, Merlot, Sangiovese
Very full-bodied: Bright bronze yellow ocher. High level of tannins, low acidity. Ex: Syrah, Malbec, Cabernet-Sauvignon

Red wine age

New Wines: Ring Fino, rich colors, more opaque, violet-red. Maximum tannins, acidity and fruit aromas.
Aged Wines: Wide ring, wide color variation, dull, transparent, red-orange. It loses acidity but gains aromas and spices.

Properties to observe in the visual phase

  • Quality: Clear, Crystalline
  • Defects: Powdery, Cloudy, Very Cloudy
  • Viscosity: Few tears, Lots of tears

The Tears of Wine

The "tear" of wine, also known as the "legs" or "bow," refers to the drops that run down the inner walls of the glass after the wine is shaken. These tears do not indicate quality, but rather physical aspects of the wine, especially alcohol content and, in some cases, residual sugar concentration.

What causes tears?

The phenomenon is explained by Marangoni effect:

  • Alcohol evaporates faster than water.
  • When the alcohol accumulates on the edge of the glass, it volatilizes, increasing the surface tension of the remaining liquid.
  • This difference causes the liquid to rise up the walls of the cup and form drops, which then run off.

Relationship with alcohol and sugar

Characteristics of the TearInterpretation
Many tearsHigher alcohol content
Few tearsLow or medium alcohol content
Flow slowlyMay indicate greater viscosity, due to alcohol or sugar
Drain quicklyLighter wine, with less alcohol or sugar

Detailed notes:

  • Alcohol is the main factor. A wine with ≥ 13.5% tends to form more tears and these flow more slowly due to the greater viscosity.
  • Residual sugar (sweet wines) also increases viscosity and contributes to thick, sluggish tears.
  • In dry wines with a low alcohol content (≤ 12%), tears are generally few, thin and quick.
  • The temperature of the wine affects the phenomenon: tears are less visible if the wine is very cold (less alcohol volatilization).
Important

Tears do not reveal aromas, flavors or balance, nor do they serve to define whether a wine is "good". They only help the taster to sensorially confirm whether there is alcohol or sugar at levels perceived by smell and taste.

Olfactory Analysis

Primary Aromas (Varietals)

Primary or Varietal aromas are very characteristic and identifiable in wines from very fragrant varieties. Among these primary aromas, the floral, fruity, mineral, or sometimes spicy series predominate.

Ex: Moscatel, Malvasia, Sauvignon Blanc, Verdelho, Riesling, Gewürztraminer

Secondary Aromas

Secondary aromas come from yeast, the transformation of sugar into alcohol or Malolactic fermentation. They are the most frequent and abundant in all wines. Among them, aromas of flowers (including honey), fruits (green, ripe or jam), spices, vegetal notes, etc.

Tertiary Aromas (Bouquet)

For Aging (tertiary aromas) or Bouquet aromas, the ranges multiply: floral, fruity, honey, jam, wood and balsamic, empyreumatic series: roasted coffee, chocolate, green pepper, cooked fruit, burnt rubber and other hot or even burning aromas, spices, animal and chemical.

White Wines

Primary aromas (coming directly from the grape and Terroir)

Fruity: Green apple, pear, peach, apricot (fresh), ananas (pineapple), passion fruit, lemon, lime, grapefruit, melon, banana (in some tropical varieties)

Florals: Orange blossom, Chamomile, Acacia, Linden, Jasmine, White rose

Herbaceous / Vegetables: Cut grass, Asparagus, Peas, Tomato leaves, Herbal tea, Green tea, Fresh eucalyptus

Minerals / Others: Chalk, Silex (flint), Wet stone, Naphtha/kerosene (especially in evolved Riesling)

Secondary aromas (resulting from fermentation processes)

Alcoholic Fermentation/Yeasts: Banana (amyl aroma, young), Fruit candy, Fresh bread, Biscuit, Brioche, Cake dough

Malolactic fermentation: Butter, Cream, Yogurt, Cream

Yeast autolysis (sparkling): Bread dough, Sliced bread, Shortbread biscuits

New wood: Vanilla, Coconut, Clove, Cinnamon, Nutmeg, Light Smoke

Tertiary aromas (developed during aging)

Evolved Fruits: Boiled apple, Dried apricot, Candied fruits, Jams

Evolved Florals: Dried flowers, withered rose

Oxidative notes: Honey, Praline, Caramel, Beeswax, Almond paste

Empireumatic / Balsamic: Burnt caramel, Coffee, Light tobacco, Cedar, Resin

Red Wines

Primary aromas (directly from the grape and Terroir)

Fruity: Strawberry, Raspberry, Cherry, Currant, Blackberry, Blueberry, Black Plum, Fresh Fig

Florals: Violet, Pink, Peony

Herbaceous / Vegetables: Green pepper, Tomato leaf, Black tea, Dried herbs, Thyme, Bay leaf

Fresh Spices: Black pepper, Licorice, Light nutmeg

Secondary aromas (arising from fermentation and aging in wood)

Malolactic fermentation: Cream, Milk, Butter (rare, more noticeable in light reds)

New and aging wood: Vanilla, Coconut, Clove, Cinnamon, Nutmeg, Cedar, Smoke, Dark chocolate, Roasted coffee, Toasted oak

Light empireumatics (from wood toast): Toasted bread, Burnt sugar, Cocoa

Tertiary aromas (coming from the evolution of the wine over time)

Evolved Fruits: Dried plum, Dried fig, Black fruit compote, Sour cherry liqueur, Raisins

Vegetables/Earths: Mushroom, Truffle, Wet forest, Earth

Animal: Leather, Raw meat, Wet fur, Dried blood, Wild game

Intense Empireumatics: Dry tobacco, Tar, Smoke, Burnt rubber, Strong coffee, Cocoa, Burnt toast

Old wood and balsams: Old Cedar, Resin, Eucalyptus, Balsam

Chemicals (at low levels or defects): Varnish, Solvent, Wax, Medicinal

Taste Analysis

The mouth must confirm the olfactory impressions. It is observed:

  • The attack: First sensation on the tongue (sweetness, alcohol, freshness)
  • The middle of the mouth: Texture, body, tannins
  • The ending: Aromatic persistence and balance

An unbalanced wine may reveal excessive sweetness, acidity or tannins without aromatic support.

Parameters to observe in taste analysis

  • Dryness or sweetness
  • Acidity
  • Alcohol
  • Astringency
  • Intensity
  • Body
  • Aftertaste

Wine Defects

Rusty Nose

All wines lose freshness and progressively gain a flat or musty smell, with smells of touched apple and sherry Fino, in the case of white wines, and bitter-sweet and caramel, in the case of red wines.

Oxidized Flavor

Just like the smell – tired, boring, harsh in the whites, sweet and sour in the reds.

Volatile

The term refers both to the smell of acetic acid (vinegar) and to what is associated with it. The two can occur simultaneously or separately.

  • Acetic acid – gives the wines the sour smell of vinegar, with a lean and sharp flavor.
  • Ethyl acetate – smells like nail polish, chemical solvents, glue. It makes the wine firm and spicy on the palate, especially at the end.

Both arise from the contamination of wine with acetic bacteria that degrade alcohol producing acetic acid and carbon dioxide.

SO₂ (Sulfur Dioxide)

SO₂ protects wine from oxygen. When it has too much presence it can be smelled, like an acrid puff from an extinguished match, and felt as pungent at the top of the nose and back of the throat. It can be dissipated by "gushing" decanting or by swirling the wine vigorously in the glass. Tolerance varies from person to person.

H₂S (Hydrogen Sulfide)

"Reduced" sulfur (combined with hydrogen) produces unpleasant smells of rotten eggs and burning rubber. If it is a mild bad smell (bottle reduction), airing may remove it. If it is extreme, it produces mercaptans, smelling like garlic, onions, sewage. Nothing to do! Produced by yeast in musts with a low nitrogen content.

With cork — TCA (Trichloroanisole)

Tri- and Tetrachloroanisole (TCA) and Tribromoanisole (TBA) have a well-known aroma: "corked", musty, musty, damp, musty.

Cause: It originates from the attack of fungi existing in natural cork stoppers to chlorine derivatives (chloroanisoles) used in washing and bleaching cork stoppers. TBA originates from fungal attack on bromine derivatives used to disinfect wood sometimes used in the manufacture of wine cellars and cellars. Both have a very low perception threshold and are one of the main defects of bottled or unbottled wine. It masks the fruit of the wine, dries it out and reduces its flavor.

It becomes more evident the longer the bottle is open.

Brett (Brettanomyces)

Aroma of leather, leather, leather goods, animal, sweat, horse, stable, pigsty, goat hair. At low levels it creates complexity.

Contamination of grapes, wine, equipment or aging barrels with yeast Brettanomyces which produce tetrahydropyridines (4-ethyl phenol, 4-vinyl phenol). Low levels of SO₂ and storage at high temperatures facilitate the occurrence.

Tasting Vocabulary: Sweetness and Acidity

Describing a wine is not just recording taste sensations: it is also choosing words that convey whether these characteristics are pleasant or undesirable, and to what extent they are balanced. The same attribute — such as sweetness or acidity — can be considered a defect or a virtue, depending on the style of the wine.

The sweetness of the wine

The perception of sweetness is directly linked to the presence of residual sugars and also to the taste perception influenced by alcohol and glycerol.

Complimentary vocabulary

When balanced with freshness and acidity, sweetness is positive: "velvety, creamy, greedy, unctuous, smooth, luscious, ripe".

  • Example: a Setúbal Moscatel can be described as "unctuous, greedy and velvety, with notes of honey and candied orange".
  • Example: a botrytized Sauternes is a "golden nectar, sweet and molten, without ever being heavy."

Pejorative vocabulary

When sweetness is excessive or misplaced, terms such as "sugary, syrupy, soft, heavy, bittersweet, saccharine".

  • Example: At an entry-level Vinho Verde, the perception of unbalanced sugar can be called "sweet" or "heavy".
Teaching tip

The word "molasses" may be a compliment in dessert wines, but a harsh criticism in a wine that should be dry.

The acidity

The acidity is Vital for the structure, freshness and longevity of the wine. It's like the backbone that supports the whole thing.

Complimentary vocabulary

Balanced wines have acidity described as "fresh, lively, nervous, vigorous".

  • Example: a Alvarinho from Monção and Melgaço can be described as "edgy and fresh, with a vibrant citrus finish".
  • Example: a Kabinett German Riesling can be praised for its "bright acidity that balances the natural sweetness."

Pejorative vocabulary

  • For lack: "soft, skinny, nerveless" wine. This happens in very mature or poorly preserved white wines.
  • For excess: Terms such as "sour, metallic, sharp, acrid, aggressive" are common in young wines from cold regions or from grapes harvested before ideal ripeness.
  • Example: a cool-climate, early-harvest red might be called "green and squeaky."

Volatile acidity

Volatile acidity results from the presence of acetic acid and other compounds. In low doses, it can give vivacity; In excess, it indicates a defect.

  • Pejorative vocabulary: "acetic, vinegary, chopped, altered".
  • Example: wine stored in poor conditions may have a "stinging nose, reminiscent of vinegar."

Vocabulary: Tannins, Texture and Saltiness

The salty feeling

The salty taste is rare, but may occur:

  • In wines from regions with mineral-rich soils (such as some Assyrtiko from Santorini)
  • In wines with a maritime influence (such as Albariños from Galicia)

Complimentary vocabulary: "salty, fresh, marine, mineral".

Example: an Atlantic Green can be described as "fresh and salty, reminiscent of a sea breeze".

Pejorative vocabulary: "detergent", when the sensation is artificial or unpleasant.

Tannins: strength and delicacy

In reds, tannins are fundamental. They Dão structure, texture and longevity.

Tannin Strength

Complimentary vocabulary: "structured, solid, fused, tasty".

Example: a Douro Reserva can be praised for its "solid structure and molten tannins."

Pejorative vocabulary (missing): "hollow, amorphous, structureless".

Pejorative vocabulary (excess): "harsh, astringent, rustic, charged".

Example: a cheap, poorly made red might be described as "rough and harsh."

Tanninous Delicacy

Complimentary vocabulary: "tannin Fino, fat that can be chewed, round".

Example: a Pinot Noir from Burgundy is classic for its fine and delicate tannins.

Pejorative vocabulary: "rough, rugged, coarse".

Example: young reds with poorly managed stems.

Tactile sensations

Comparing the texture of wines to fabrics helps convey the tactile sensation left in the mouth.

Complimentary vocabulary: "silk, velvet, satin, cotton" → refer to softness and pleasure.

Example: a Merlot from Pomerol can be described as "soft as velvet".

Pejorative vocabulary: "wool, jute, twill" → refer to rusticity or unpleasant dryness.

Example: a low-cost red wine, with green tannins, can be compared to "jute".

The aromatic quality in the mouth

Even after swallowing, some wines leave a long aromatic persistence (P.A.L.).

Complimentary vocabulary: "ripe, elegant vegetable, vanilla, woody flavor".

Example: an oaked Chardonnay can be said to be "ripe, vanilla and tasty with well-integrated wood".

Pejorative vocabulary: "brandy flavor, stem flavor", which indicate failure.

Example: in a young red wine with poorly managed stems, it can be said that it has a "stem flavor, raw vegetable".

Conclusion

Wine vocabulary is a precision instrument: it allows you to communicate not only what you feel, but also how you evaluate that sensation.

  • A wine can be sweet and velvety (compliment in a liqueur), but also sweet and cloying (criticism in a wine that should be dry).
  • The acidity can be fresh and nervous (a virtue in young whites), but also acidic and squeaky (a defect in green reds).
  • Tannins can be silky like velvet (in great wines), or rough like jute (in poorly vinified wines).

This wealth of expressions expands the taster's repertoire, helping to communicate the experience in a clear, technical and even poetic way.

How to Use the Terms

  • The same term can be praise or criticism, depending on the context.
  • "Melado" → positive in Moscatel, negative in Vinho Verde.
  • "Cut" → positive in a fresh white, negative in an unbalanced red.
  • Always compare the chosen word to the type of wine and expected style.
