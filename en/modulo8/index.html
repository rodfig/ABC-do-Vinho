Bottle shapes are not just aesthetic: they communicate tradition, type of wine and even production method. Knowing each shape is an essential part of reading the bottle before serving.

Pronounced shoulders, cylindrical body. Classic shape for Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Bordeaux blends; favors long corks and facilitates Decanting

Why does form matter in service?

A Bordeaux bottle with pronounced shoulders is ideal for wine with sediment: the shoulders act as a natural barrier that retains the lees during Decanting. A Burgundy bottle, without shoulders, does not offer this function — Burgundy wines tend to be less sedimented and do not need it.

Large formats and aging

A Magnum ages more slowly than a standard bottle, because the volume/cork surface ratio is more favorable: less oxygen enters per unit of wine. The larger the format, the slower the development — which is why large formats are preferred for very long-lived wines aging.

Cork comes from cork oak — Quercus suber —, typical tree of the western Mediterranean, with a special presence in Portugal, Spain and North Africa. It is a unique material because it is harvested without cutting down the tree: the bark regenerates after each extraction, making cork a renewable resource with low environmental impact.

Longevity
The cork oak can live for more than 100 to 200 years and is harvested cyclically throughout its life
1st harvest
It occurs around 25 years of age; The cork from this first extraction is called virgin cork and is not used in stoppers — it does not yet have the necessary quality
Later cycle
Repeats approximately every 9 to 12 years, depending on region and growing conditions
Suberina
Main component of cork; responsible for the impermeability and elasticity that allow the cork to seal the bottle for years
Other compounds
Cellulose and lignin complete the structure; the combination provides lightness, compressive strength and chemical stability
Portugal and cork

Portugal is the world's largest producer and processor of cork, responsible for more than half of global production. The cork oak is the Portuguese national tree and its sustainable exploitation is regulated — cork cannot be harvested from trees less than 25 years old or before the minimum period between extractions has elapsed.

The market currently offers several closure solutions, each with distinct advantages depending on the style of wine and the desired time age-worthy. The choice of closure is a technical and commercial decision made by the producer.

Natural cork (1 piece)
Made from a single piece of natural cork. Typical dimensions: 38×24, 45×24 or 50×24 mm (length × diameter). It is the traditional closure for age-worthy and premium wines — allows slow and differentiated micro-oxygenation
Clogged cork
Natural cork with pores filled with cork powder and natural resin to improve appearance and uniformity; maintains natural properties but reduces visual defects
Agglomerated cork
Made from cork granules (2–5 mm) held together by a binder; lower cost; suitable for wines age-worthy short or quick consumption
Technical / composite stopper
Granulated with natural cork disc on the tops (TwinTop); natural-like appearance, intermediate cost, more predictable behavior
Capsulated corks / stoppers
Corks with outer capsule for easy extraction and reinsertion; used in spirits, liqueurs and wines for frequent consumption
Screw cap (Stelvin)
Screwable aluminum capsule; excellent sealing and precise control of O₂ entry; increasingly accepted in wines whites and rosés for young consumption
Vinolok / glass stopper
Glass lid with silicone or PVDC gasket; premium and reusable solution, used in high-end segments
Screw-cap: prejudice vs. reality

The screw-cap is often associated with low quality wines, but this perception is mistaken. New Zealand and Australia have widely adopted it for top wines, especially Sauvignon Blanc and Riesling, where preserving aromatic freshness is a priority. The screw-cap eliminates the risk of TCA and offers perfectly controllable sealing.

The Espumante stopper is a piece of specialized engineering: it must withstand an internal pressure of 5–6 bar and maintain its seal for months or years age-worthy in the cellar. Its construction is different from the tranquil cork.

Structure
Compressed granular body (cork agglomerate) with two or more discs of solid natural cork at the ends that contact the wine — thus, the part that touches the liquid is always made of quality cork
Mushroom shape
The part of the cork that remains inside the bottle is subject to pressure throughout the cellaring period; when extracted, the compressed area expands progressively — this is the characteristic mushroom of the Espumante corks used
Cage (muselet)
Wire twisted into six turns that holds the cork in the neck; integral part of the security system of any Espumante
Quality classes
Extra, superior, 1st and 2nd types, depending on the material, disc thickness and finish; determine the price and performance of the cork
Why 6 laps of the cage?

The convention of six twists in the metal cage dates back to the time of Napoleon Bonaparte, who reportedly asked the Champagne producer to fasten the cage as quickly as possible — six twists became the industry standard and remains the same today, even when one or two would technically be sufficient.

TCA — Cork Flavor

TCA
Trichloroanisole
Molecule responsible for the "cork flavor"
ng/L
Detection threshold
Nanograms per liter — extremely low
Chlorine
Main precursor
Avoid in places where it comes into contact with cork

"Cork flavor" or "wine bouchonné" is one of the most well-known faults in wine service. The molecule responsible, trichloroanisole (TCA), is detectable by the human sense of smell in concentrations of nanograms per liter — an extraordinary sensitivity that makes contamination easy to identify even in tiny doses.

Characteristic smell
Mold, damp cardboard, attic, mildew. May suppress fruity aromas even without a noticeable "cork" odor — "flat" or fruitless wines may be contaminated
Origin of the TCA
It results from the transformation of natural phenols present in cork (or in the cellar environment) by the action of microorganisms in the presence of chlorinated compounds (cleaning chlorine, chlorinated fungicides)
Prevention in the basement
Avoid chlorine-based cleaning products in areas in contact with corks and Madeira; use chlorine-free disinfectants; control humidity to limit fungal growth
In service
If the wine has a TCA odor, reject and record — the wine is not dangerous to health but is defective. Inform the customer without drama and offer replacement
TCA doesn't just come from the cork

Although the popular name is "cork flavor", TCA can contaminate wines sealed with a screw-cap or bag-In-box when the cellar environment is contaminated. The molecule can be in wood, in the concrete of vats, in Madeira pallets — any material in contact with chlorine and microorganisms is a potential vector.

The wine opening and service protocol reflects respect for the wine, the producer and the customer. It is not an empty ritual: each step has a technical or hospitality reason. The professional Sommelier performs the sequence in a fluid and discreet way.

Opening Sequence

1. Presentation of the bottle
Approach the customer who placed the order on the right side; show the label (wine name, harvest/year, producer, region). Confirm that it is the wine ordered before opening
2. Position
Keep the bottle vertical before opening; When opening, hold firmly with the label facing the customer — the presentation does not end with opening
3. Cut the capsule
Crop below the ring (marisa/merisa) so that the wine, when served, does not come into contact with the cut capsule; remove the capsule and clean the neck with a clean, odorless cloth
4. Extracting the cork
Use lever corkscrew (Sommelier) — the professional standard. Insert the spiral until only one spiral is visible; use the lever to extract gently, without piercing the cork all the way (avoids particles)
5. Cork examination
Check the physical condition of the cork (intact, no mold, no deterioration). Smell briefly. The tradition of showing the cork to the customer is falling into disuse — today most sommeliers directly offer to taste the wine
6. Customer proof
Pour a small dose into the host's (or whoever asked) glass; wait for approval. If the wine is in order, proceed with service — on the right side, in order of courtesy (traditionally: ladies, seniors, host last)

Essential Material for Service

  • glass of tasting suitable for the wine
  • Clean, odorless service cloth
  • Lever Corkscrew (Sommelier/laguiole)
  • Sage or plate for the cork
  • Decanter (when necessary), candle or flashlight for Decanting
  • Frapé / bucket of ice and water for sparkling wines and whites
  • birthplace / bottle holder (old wines with sediment)

Decanting is not just an aesthetic or protocol issue. There are two distinct technical reasons that justify Decanting, and confusing the two leads to service errors.

Two Reasons to Decant

1. Separate sediment
Wines reds age-worthy and some old vintages deposit lees and tartrate crystals; decant slowly with light behind the neck so as not to transfer the sediment to the glass
2. Aerate the wine
Young, tannic and full-bodied wines benefit from oxygenation to soften tannins and open aromas that are still closed (reductive); the air contact surface on Decanter accelerates this process

Practical Procedure

  • Leave the bottle in an upright position 24 hours beforehand (if possible) so that the sediment settles at the bottom
  • Use a light behind the bottleneck during Decanting: the candle is traditional and scenic, but a flashlight gives more effective light and avoids odors; stop pouring when the sediment starts to approach the bottleneck
  • Pour slowly in a continuous stream — sudden movements resuspend the sediment

Times of Decanting by Style

reds young and tannic
30 minutes to 2 hours; very young and robust wines sometimes up to 3 hours
reds aged and mature
5 to 30 minutes only — the main objective is to separate the sediment, not to aerate it; Too much oxygen destroys a ripe wine quickly
Wines whites
Rarely decanted; exception for very complex and long whites aging (e.g. old Montrachet) which benefit from 15–20 minutes
Decanter broad-based vs. narrow base

The wide base maximizes the air contact surface — ideal for young wine that requires intense aeration. The narrow base (more like a carboy) limits contact with air — recommended for separating sediments in old wines without aerating them excessively.

Opening a Espumante is a visible and high-risk moment: the internal pressure is equivalent to that of a bus tire. The correct technique is silent and controlled — exactly the opposite of the "rocket cork" effect you see on television.

Classical Opening (without sabrage)

1. Cool the bottle
Ice bucket with water and ice in a 1:1 ratio for 20–30 minutes; serve at 6–8 °C depending on style — cold bottle = lower pressure and less risk of overflow
2. Remove capsule and release cage
Remove the metal foil and untwist the cage six turns; Always keep one hand on the cork throughout the process — the cork may come out on its own with pressure
3. Controlled extraction
Hold the cork and cage with one hand and gently rotate the base of the bottle with the other; the cork comes out with a "sigh" — not a bang; Tilting the bottle at 45° facilitates the process and reduces foam production

Sabrage — the saber ritual

Sabrage is a spectacular ritual of Napoleonic origins: legend has it that cavalry officers opened Champagne bottles with their sabers while riding horses.

Technique
Very cold bottle; remove the foil and cage; hold firmly with your thumb on the punt; tilt the bottle at 45° with the seam facing up; slide the back of the saber along the seam until the lip of the neck and give a dry, firm blow
What happens
The internal pressure breaks the neck ring (marisa) cleanly — the glass goes with the cork. If the bottle is cold enough, glass rarely gets inside.
Warning
It should only be carried out by trained people — there is a real risk of injury from flying glass; never point the bottle at people or windows

Basic Service Rules

The serving rules are not arbitrary — they follow the logic of taste progression: starting with the simplest and lightest, progressing to the most complex and full-bodied. The exception can always occur when the customer prefers another order, but the Sommelier must know the rules to adapt them with discretion.

  • Always respect the customer’s individual taste — rules are guidelines, not impositions
  • Serve whites before reds
  • Serve lighter wines before fuller ones
  • Serve dry wines before sweet ones
  • Serve young wines before aged ones
  • If a wine is used in the preparation of a delicacy, it must accompany the meal — there is a natural affinity
  • If only one wine accompanies the entire meal, choose a versatile style (e.g.: red medium body, firm acidity)
  • If only one Espumante accompanies the meal, prefer a raw (dry) style
  • Avoid too much variety of drinks in a single meal — it confuses the palate and dilutes the experience
Role of Sommelier in service management

A long letter can intimidate and confuse the customer. It is up to Sommelier to facilitate the choice, adapting suggestions to personal taste, meal style and budget — without ever making the customer feel like they made a bad choice. The best suggestion is always the one that the customer accepts with pleasure.

glass is not a mere container — it shapes the aromatic and gustatory expression of the wine. The temperature of the wine in glass, the way in which the aromas are concentrated, and the surface in contact with the tongue vary with the shape of the glass. An identical wine, served in differently shaped glasses, may appear to be two different wines.

Features of glass Ideal

Material
Smooth, colorless, patternless, dye-free glass — allows correct visual assessment of color and clarity
Foot
Essential; holding it by the foot (never by the bowl) avoids heating the wine with the heat of your hand and leaves the bowl clean for visual assessment
Shape
The bowl must be wider than the mouth — the narrower opening concentrates the volatile aromas that rise from the wine; a mouth wider than the bowl disperses aromas before reaching the nose
Size
Larger glasses, with more air space above the wine, favor aromatic evolution; Smaller, narrower glasses preserve the bubbles in sparkling wines

Temperatures and Cups by Style

rosés

reds young and fruity
glass more open (for ventilation in the bowl); serve between 14 and 17 °C
reds age-worthy and full-bodied
glass large with wide cup (Bordeaux or Burgundy type, according to variety); serve between 17 and 20 °C
Sparkling wines

A visually clean glass may still contain detergent residue, grease or cabinet smells that directly interfere with the evaluation of the wine — especially the aromas. The hygiene of the glass is part of the service protocol.

Wash

  • Hot water and mild unscented detergent; rinse thoroughly to remove all detergent residue
  • Dry with a clean, odorless microfiber cloth — never a cotton cloth with a laundry smell
  • For persistent dirt or limescale marks, diluted white vinegar or isopropyl alcohol used punctually are effective; followed by rigorous rinsing
  • Avoid dishwasher for premium serving glasses — high temperatures and industrial detergents dull the crystal and leave residue

Storage

  • In a restaurant: hang by the foot on an inverted rack — prevents dust from accumulating inside and makes serving easier
  • At home: store in a closed cabinet, preferably inverted; If the cupboard smells (varnish, Madeira, paper), the glasses absorb it — rinse before using
  • Avoid places close to the kitchen, spices, cleaning products or any source of strong aromas.
Glass testing before service

Professionally, before an important service, glasses are polished with a linen cloth damp with steam — a classic Sommelier technique that removes limescale stains, clouds and fingerprints, leaving the glass crystal clear. The cloth is steamed over a jug of hot water and then used quickly while warm.

The wine program à glass is today one of the pillars of modern wine list management. Well executed, it benefits the customer, the restaurant and the commercial margin — the condition is that it is supported by the correct preservation system.

Advantages for the Customer

  • Possibility to try premium or rare labels at an affordable price (fraction of the bottle price)
  • Discover new wines, regions and varieties without a full bottle commitment
  • pair with each course of a meal with different wines
  • Flexibility for those who drink little or are alone

Advantages for the Establishment

  • Increases wine sales per meal — more points of sale per table
  • Stock turnover: slow-moving labels that would otherwise remain stationary are used up
  • With Coravin, top bottles enter the program at glass without risk of waste
  • Enomatic allows you to display open bottles for days without loss of quality, with an attractive presentation
Price and portion management

The margin on a glass program depends on strict portion control. The standard dose is 150 ml (1/5 of a bottle), but it may vary. The price for glass should cover the cost of the bottle in 4–5 doses, plus the service margin and the cost of any losses. Without an adequate preservation system, the waste of opened bottles can eliminate the program's entire margin.

The cellar is the heart of the wine aging. Its environment must be carefully controlled so that each bottle evolves in a balanced and healthy way, respecting the conditions that the producer defined when bottling.

Temperature

The storage temperature must be between 12 °C and 15 °C so that constant — Sudden fluctuations are more harmful than a temperature slightly above ideal.

  • Too high a temperature: accelerates maturation and can cause premature oxidation; wines "cook" prematurely
  • Too low temperature: prevents normal evolution; at temperatures close to 0 °C precipitation of tartaric salts may occur (harmless but undesirable crystals in glass)

Humidity

The humidity level must remain between 65% and 75%, avoiding both the corks drying out and excess mold.

  • Excess humidity: spread sand and pebbles on the soil, or use low-cost calcium chloride devices to absorb moisture
  • Too dry environment: periodically spray water on the floor, or place a basin with water and charcoal to maintain stability

Other Essential Conditions

Bottle position
Store horizontally: the cork remains in contact with the wine, preventing dehydration and maintaining elasticity and correct sealing
Light
The basement must be dark and protected from direct or intense lighting; UV light degrades aromatic compounds and accelerates oxidation — that's why many premium bottles are made of dark glass
Vibration and vibration
Shelves protected against constant vibrations; if the basement is close to subways, boilers or engines, install rubber supports and flexible fittings
Smells
The basement must be isolated from strong smells — spices, onion, garlic, chlorine; these odors penetrate through the cork and contaminate the wine
Control and registration
Thermometer and hygrometer fixed to the wall for constant monitoring; maintenance of Book of Garrafeira — record of entries, exits, position, vintages and conservation status

The day cellar is the practical extension of the general cellar. While the general cellar is the extended age-worthy space, the day cellar must contain the wines in active rotation for immediate service — already at the correct temperature, ready to go to the table without waiting.

Cave of the Day

  • Reduced quantities, adjusted to expected daily demand — avoid excessive rotation that forces bottles to abrupt temperature changes
  • Wines already at the correct serving temperature: whites and cold sparkling wines, reds at controlled room temperature
  • Use of multi-zone climate-controlled refrigerated cabinets (whites at 6–10 °C, reds at 14–18 °C) is the most effective and professional solution

Organization of Garrafeira General

The storage logic must comply with the natural conditions of the space and facilitate stock management:

Lower shelves
whites and sparkling wines — closer to soil where the temperature is lower and constant
Central shelves
reds — intermediate temperature zone
Bottom shelves / more stable
Wines age-worthy and reserves — where temperature variation is smaller and access is less frequent
Easy access shelves
Bottles for immediate consumption and greater rotation — close to the exit, in an easily visible location
Book of Garrafeira

The systematic registration of Garrafeira is the most important management tool for a professional cellar. It must include: wine reference, producer, region, harvest, quantity entered, date of arrival, location on the shelf, tasting notes and expected consumption date. Without registration, visibility over stock is lost and the risk of consuming wines before the optimum point — or too late — increases.

The vast majority of wines produced in the world are made for immediate consumption — young, fresh, within 1 to 3 years. Storing a wine that doesn't have the structure to do so doesn't improve it: it deteriorates it. The criteria for selecting age-worthy wines are based on objective factors.

Criteria age-worthy

Structure (tannin)
In reds, tannin acts as a natural antioxidant — wines with firm, well-integrated tannins age more gracefully
Acidity
In whites (and in all styles), acidity preserves freshness and balance; whites with low acidity ages poorly
Residual sugar
High-quality sweet wines (e.g. Sauternes, Riesling Auslese/TBA) have extraordinary longevity — balanced sugar and acidity act as preservatives
Production quality
A well-made wine, from a rigorous producer, with ripe but balanced grapes, has a better aging potential than a wine with a high yield and of average quality.

whites to Save

  • High quality Chardonnay with aging to Madeira (Burgundy, Viognier from large producer)
  • Sémillon (e.g. Hunter Valley, Bordeaux Blanc)
  • High-acid Riesling (Germany — Spätlese, Auslese and higher)
  • Quality Chenin Blanc (Loire — Vouvray, Savennières)
  • Alvarinho/Albariño from top producers with intent age-worthy

reds to Save

  • Cabernet Sauvignon (Bordeaux, Napa Valley)
  • Touriga Nacional (wines from Douro and Alentejo from reference producers)
  • Nebbiolo: Barolo and Barbaresco (Piedmont)
  • Great Bordeaux wines from the best vintages
  • Wines with aging extended to Madeira of quality

Cold wine vs. mulled wine

A wine served too cold loses aromatic expression — the volatile compounds do not evaporate easily — and highlights excessive tannins or acidity, making it harsh. A wine served too hot becomes alcoholic (ethanol evaporates more), excessively sweet and heavy. The correct temperature is the balance point between expression and freshness.

Wines like Moscatel, intensely aromatic and sweet, should be served very cold, to control sugar and alcohol. They pair well with strong cheeses, such as Queijo de Azeitão.

The wine list is a strategic tool — it must reflect both the restaurant's identity and the customer's profile. A good letter is not the longest; it is the most coherent, well explained and appropriate to the cuisine served.

Structuring the Charter

  1. Organization by regions and countries of origin (DO, DOC, IGP, etc.) — or by style, depending on the restaurant's profile
  2. Grouping by main varieties when the approach is varietalistic (e.g.: Chardonnay, Antão Vaz, Touriga Nacional)
  3. Arrangement of the most affordable to the most expensive within each category
  4. Pairing with gastronomy — the menu must complement the menu, not exist independently
  5. Brief explanations about regions, countries and varieties — the customer is informed before ordering and trusts suggestions more
  6. Indication of the producer or winery of each wine
  7. Harvest and bottle capacity clearly identified
  8. Photographs of the labels (optional) — increase customer recognition and trust
  9. Space for tasting notes (in digital or special menu formats)

Function of Sommelier in the Charter

An extensive letter can confuse and paralyze the customer due to the abundance of options. The Sommelier must:

  • Get an in-depth understanding of each wine listed — including current harvests and recent tasting notes
  • Make suggestions adapted to the customer's declared taste, the chosen dish and the budget
  • Never make the customer feel that their choice is wrong — guide without imposing
  • Update the menu regularly, removing out-of-stock wines and introducing new information

In daily service and basement management, professionals are faced with failures that they need to know how to identify, communicate to the customer without alarm and resolve efficiently. Most problems have clear causes and direct solutions.

Wine Failures

Cork flavor (TCA)
Musty odor, damp tobacco, wet cardboard. It can also suppress fruity aromas without a noticeable "corked" smell. Solution: reject the wine, report the fault to the supplier, offer a new bottle to the customer without drama
Oxidation
Aromas of cooked apple, syrup, rancid nuts; brownish color; loss of freshness and fruit. It can result from poor corking, incorrect storage or a bottle that has been opened for too long. Solution: store better, serve quickly after opening
Reduction
Aromas of rotten eggs, rubber, burnt straw — the result of a lack of oxygen during winemaking or aging in very reductive conditions. Often correctable: shaking the wine, decanting or aerating it solves it in a few minutes
Unwanted refermentation
Bubbles in a still wine, cloudiness, slightly carbonated flavor. Indicates bacterial or yeast activity after bottling. Defective wine - reject

Basement Problems

Bottle weeping
Traces of wine around the cork or in the capsule — indicates that the wine has overflowed due to excessive temperature or sudden variations. The cork may be compromised and the wine may be oxidized. Check and consume shortly
Mold in the cork
External mold on the cork or capsule — a sign of excessive humidity or insufficient ventilation in the cellar. External mold does not necessarily contaminate the wine, but it is a sign of poor conservation; clean the bottle and check the wine before serving
Tartrate Crystals
whites (whites) or reddish (reds) crystals on the bottom or in the cork — are harmless and are a sign of lack of tartaric stabilization. They do not affect the quality of the wine; inform the customer calmly
Sediment
Normal in quality old or unfiltered wines — decant before serving; present to the customer as an indicator age-worthy and quality, not as a defect
Report failures to the customer

When a wine fails, the Sommelier must act with discretion and efficiency: identify the failure without hesitation, remove the wine without drama and immediately offer an alternative. Never argue with the customer about whether the failure is real — if the customer feels something is wrong, it is wrong for them. Hospitality always trumps technical debate.
